# Gratitude

Deep in the gentle green countryside of dear old England stands an Old Rectory, where the local priest of those parts once made his home.

The house is surrounded by beautiful grounds so thoroughly steeped in magic that, if you listen very carefully indeed, you can sometimes hear the fairies and elves whispering in the woods. Admittedly, the leaves of the ancient trees make almost exactly the same sound, but this merely demonstrates how accomplished the fairies and elves have become at whispering.

Those woods are very old. Some of the oaks remember Guy Fawkes hiding among their roots, although historians maintain that he was somewhere else entirely. Several magnificent beeches, birches and ashes claim to have watched Christopher Columbus sail the ocean blue, despite being many miles from the sea. Trees are notoriously unreliable witnesses, but they tell excellent stories.

Beyond the woods lies a pond filled with lilies as tall as the Old Rectory itself. On hot summer days, fairies gather beneath their resplendent leaves to escape the sun. The fennel grows taller than you or me and, when freshly picked, smells of the aniseed you first tasted when you were tiny. Lavender borders the paths and perfumes the house, while its scent hangs in the warm air wherever you walk.

Many generations of moles have also lived in these grounds.

The moles are extremely fond of digging tunnels. They have constructed roads, crossroads, chambers, shortcuts and at least one underground roundabout, although nobody has yet established who has the right of way. Unfortunately, all the earth removed during these ambitious public works has to go somewhere. It generally appears in the middle of the gardener’s lawns.

The present gardener does not mind too much, provided the moles do not become overenthusiastic. He has trees to tend, fennel to pick for his salads and lavender to gather for the Old Rectory. The lavender perfumes the lobby, makes an excellent oil for soothing burns and leaves visitors with the impression that the entire house has recently been polished by fairies.

The molehills are useful too. Their dark, finely worked earth makes excellent topsoil for the shrubs around the garden.

For their part, the moles are grateful that someone cares for the woods, pond and lawns beneath which they conduct their extensive tunnelling operations. The gardener appreciates the way they aerate the soil and keep the worm population in balance. The moles eat some worms, the worms improve the earth, the earth nourishes the garden and the garden provides a home for them all.

Most of the time, therefore, gardener and moles live together in a state of mutual respect.

Most of the time.

The Old Rectory has also known gardeners—and moles—who were rather less grateful.

Soon after the first mole family moved to the Old Rectory, many generations ago, the gardener of the time became convinced that their molehills were destroying his magnificent lawns.

He tried stamping the earth flat.

The following morning, three new molehills appeared.

He tried filling the tunnels with stones.

The moles politely pushed them back out.

He placed heavy flowerpots over every entrance he could find. By sunrise, the flowerpots had moved several feet across the lawn and somebody had planted mushrooms in them.

The gardener suspected the elves.

He was correct, but proving it would have required an inquiry of such length and complexity that he decided to concentrate upon the moles.

Fortunately, this was long before gunpowder became readily available. Unable to obtain modern high-velocity weaponry, the gardener armed himself with a pickaxe and constructed an enormous catapult beside the potting shed.

From dawn until dusk, he lay in wait.

Whenever a fresh mound of earth appeared, he charged across the lawn, swinging the pickaxe above his head and shouting words that respectable gardeners ordinarily reserve for bindweed. He would then load the catapult and unleash a volley of stones upon what he had come to regard as little incarnations of the devil.

The moles were always somewhere else.

His campaign soon became the most popular entertainment in the grounds. Rabbits gathered beneath the hedges. Squirrels watched from the branches. Badgers brought refreshments. Even the fairies abandoned their dances to see whether the gardener might accidentally catapult himself into the pond.

One or two mischievous elves began digging additional holes merely to keep the performance moving.

The gardener did not hit a single mole. He did, however, destroy two wheelbarrows, a sundial and an ornamental statue that had offended nobody.

If you listen carefully on a summer evening, you can still hear the magpies cackling about it.

Eventually, the strain drove him completely insane, and he was taken away for a lengthy period of rehabilitation.

This proved extremely fortunate.

During his recovery, the gardener devoted himself to finding a substance through which no mole could possibly tunnel. After years of secret experiments with stone, sand, water and several ingredients he refused to identify, he invented concrete.

Concrete soon appeared beneath houses, roads, bridges and garden sheds throughout the land. The gardener became fabulously wealthy and spent his remaining years in a mansion built entirely upon a vast concrete slab.

He was never troubled by moles again.

He did, however, remain a little nervous of lawns.

Another gardener arrived at the Old Rectory around the time of the Second Great War. She, too, believed that a perfect lawn should contain no molehills.

Her methods, however, were rather more advanced than those of the Concrete Oligarch.

She did not own a catapult. She had chemicals.

The gardener sprayed the grass with herbicides, scattered pesticides beneath the hedges and poured poisonous concoctions into every tunnel she found. Her ambition was not merely to remove the moles. She wanted a lawn upon which no crawling, burrowing, nibbling or otherwise inconvenient creature would dare to set foot.

The grass looked magnificent.

Almost everything else began to die.

Worms disappeared from the earth. Beetles vanished from beneath the stones. Wildflowers withered at the edges of the lawn, and the birds found fewer reasons to visit.

This greatly distressed the fairies. They are, after all, guardians of Mother Nature and cannot bear to watch flora and fauna wilt beneath a sea of nasty sprays. The excessive use of such chemicals is one reason that fairies are so rarely seen in English gardens nowadays.

Back then, however, poisonous chemicals were still a newfound fad, and the fairies believed something could be done.

They called an emergency meeting beneath the oldest beech tree.

Suggestions included stealing the gardener’s bottles, replacing their contents with elderflower cordial and transforming her into a slug. The first plan was judged impractical, the second a waste of perfectly good cordial and the third was rejected after the slugs complained that they had standards.

At last, one fairy remembered that the government had issued the Old Rectory with regulation gas masks.

That night, a raiding party entered the house through an upstairs window and smuggled one of the masks into the woods, together with a pair of protective gloves.

The following morning, the eldest mole received a detailed demonstration. The gas mask covered most of his body, the gloves were considerably larger than he was and nobody could understand a word he said once he put everything on.

Nevertheless, he was delighted.

The moles established a rota and returned to their tunnels with renewed vigour. One wore the protective equipment at the digging face while the others waited at a safe distance for their turn.

At first, their efforts produced the opposite result to the one intended. Each new molehill sent the gardener hurrying back to her shed for stronger chemicals. She sprayed, scattered and poured increasingly alarming substances across the grounds.

The lawn glistened.

Beneath it, a masked and gloved mole tunnelled on.

The gardener could not understand it. She consulted books, mixed new formulas and spent entire nights watching the grass. Still the molehills appeared. Some were larger than before. One was shaped rather unmistakably like a rude gesture.

The fairies denied responsibility.

Eventually, after a long-drawn-out battle of wits, the gardener admitted defeat. She packed her bags and found employment testing toilet detergents, where her enthusiasm for powerful chemicals was considered a professional virtue.

She might have left the Old Rectory a bitter and broken woman, suffering from a nasty cough and several complaints involving her kidneys. Fortunately, the visiting children gave her one final reason to feel appreciated.

The children frequently took a special midnight walk through the woods. It was ever-so-very-exciting, but all except the bravest found it just-a-little-bit-scary because the woods were oh-so-very-dark and no torches were allowed.

Shortly before the gardener departed, they asked her to lead the way.

She believed they had chosen her because she was oh-so-very-much-respected.

The truth was that years of exposure to her chemical mixtures had left her emitting a faint green glow. On a moonless night, she was considerably more useful than a lantern.

On the tearful morning of her departure, the children presented her with a card. Its cover bore a drawing of a strange luminescent creature surrounded by trees. Inside, one child had written in their very best seven-year-old felt-tip:

“How are we going to see in the woods now?”

The gardener treasured that card for the rest of her life.

The gas mask remained at the Old Rectory. Once the chemical campaign ended, the moles returned it to the potting shed, although they kept the gloves for washing up.

Many years later, a wren built her nest inside the mask.

The present gardener discovered her just as he was about to clear the shed. He closed the door again and left Mother Wren to raise her brood in peace. For six weeks, he told anyone willing to listen that he had been evicted from his own shed by a bird living in wartime protective equipment.

He sounded extremely proud of it.

Mother Wren returned to the gas mask in the years that followed. Three generations of her family were raised inside it. Only after the nest had been empty for a very long time did the gardener carefully remove it and take it to the little room at the top of the gardens.

Gardeners, however, were not the only creatures capable of forgetting that the Old Rectory belonged to everyone.

The moles occasionally forgot it too.

Children continued to visit the Old Rectory. They explored the woods, searched beneath stones, peered into the pond and played games that involved a great deal of running, hiding and shouting.

During one particularly epic game of Manhunt, a child—who shall remain nameless—accidentally trod upon the tail of a young mole.

The child never even realised what he had done.

The mole realised immediately.

He leapt from beneath the leaves with a squeal, examined his flattened tail and declared the incident an act of war.

His father attempted to reason with him.

“Accidents happen,” he said. “Especially when small children and small animals occupy the same patch of ground.”

The young mole was unmoved.

“He trod on my tail.”

“He didn’t see you.”

“He should have looked.”

“You were hiding beneath a leaf.”

“That is no excuse.”

From that day forward, the mole devoted himself to revenge.

He began by stealing a shiny knife he found in the woods. Using it as a spade, he dug an ever-so-small trench across one of the garden paths. It was not deep enough to attract the attention of an adult, but it was exactly deep enough to catch the foot of a running child.

During the next game of Manhunt, the boy tripped over it and broke his arm.

Everyone assumed he had fallen clumsily.

Beneath a nearby shrub, the young mole felt that justice had been served.

Some time later, he discovered another knife. This one was bright red and especially well suited to malicious excavation. He dug a second trench, and the same unfortunate child fell again, this time badly cutting his knee.

The child blamed himself.

The mole began planning a third trap.

Such episodes might have continued indefinitely had an angel not witnessed the latest incident.

The angel descended into the garden, seized the guilty mole by one ear and frog-marched him home to stand trial before his father.

“Did you dig those trenches?” his father asked.

The young mole studied his claws.

“Perhaps.”

“Using knives that belonged to the children?”

“They left them in the woods.”

“That does not make them yours.”

“One of them trod on my tail.”

His father gave him the severe look that fathers of every species reserve for arguments they have already heard too many times.

“Without the visiting children, there would be no need for a gardener,” he said. “Without the gardener, the woods, pond and lawns beneath which we tunnel would fall into disrepair. The children enjoy these grounds, and their enjoyment gives the humans a reason to care for them.”

The young mole muttered something about his tail.

“You chose to hide beneath a leaf during a game of Manhunt.”

“It was an excellent hiding place.”

“Evidently not.”

His father sentenced him to six months of washing up. This left the mole with very little time for digging traps and made excellent use of the protective gloves his family had inherited from the chemical war.

The angel took possession of the stolen knives.

Angels, however, do not simply return lost objects. First, they must satisfy themselves that the intended recipients are worthy of such a grand gesture. If the children continue to love the woods, care for the creatures within them and pay attention to even the smallest seedlings, they may one day be reunited with their favourite knives.

The affair troubled the inhabitants of the Old Rectory. It suggested that the old understanding was being forgotten again—not by a gardener this time, but by some of the creatures who shared the grounds with him.

Word of the young mole’s conduct eventually reached the Elven King.

He listened to the angel’s account without interruption. When she had finished, he rested both hands upon his ancient yew staff and considered what she had told him.

The mole’s revenge had been stopped, but the King feared that the same ingratitude might have taken root elsewhere.

“We must discover whether others have forgotten what it means to share these grounds,” he said. “Call them together.”

Messengers set out across the grounds.

Wrens carried the summons through the hedges. Rabbits passed it between their burrows. High above the woods, a kestrel repeated the message in a piercing call that sounded remarkably like a police officer’s whistle.

Late one night, long after the human folk had gone to bed, animals and small folk gathered before an old cave in the woods. Rabbits sat upright among the ferns. Birds crowded the branches overhead. Foxes, badgers, mice, moles and hedgehogs gathered beneath them, while fairies shone between the leaves like tiny lanterns.

The young mole stood beside his father, wearing the protective gloves and carrying a bowl that still required washing.

The Elven King was waiting for them. He struck the ground three times with his ancient yew staff.

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

The King looked across the gathering.

The clearing fell silent.

“We share these grounds with the human folk,” he began. “Most of them cannot see us. Some do not believe we exist. A regrettable few believe that gardens belong exclusively to them.”

An elderly badger muttered something about the Concrete Oligarch.

“Precisely,” said the King.

He rested both hands upon his staff.

“Yet the present gardener is not the Concrete Oligarch. He tends the woods, protects the pond and permits the moles to rearrange his lawns with only a moderate amount of grumbling.”

Several moles nodded approvingly.

“It has come to my attention, however, that some among us may have forgotten what it means to share these grounds. Before I make any judgement, I will hear your grievances.”

At first, nobody moved.

Then an old rat with a rather ruddy nose stepped into the centre of the clearing.

“The gardener stole my wine jugs!”

The rat suffered from headaches so frequently that the animals were unaccustomed to hearing him speak above a whisper. His accusation therefore caused considerable alarm.

“How many jugs?” the King asked.

“Three.”

“Were they full?”

The rat drew himself upright.

“That is not relevant.”

A murmur spread through the gathering.

“He is a thief,” the rat continued. “I demand the immediate return of my property—and anything that may remain inside the jugs.”

This produced a louder murmur.

Mother Wren flew down from the branches and landed upon a stone. She knocked her beak sharply against it until the animals gave her their attention.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

Indeed, if you hear a wren making such a sound in your garden, she may be attempting to call a public meeting.

“The gardener stole my favourite nest,” she cried.

The animals gasped.

“Your favourite?” asked a squirrel.

“The one inside the gas mask. Three generations of my family were raised in it.”

“Disgraceful,” said the squirrel.

“Outrageous,” agreed the rat, who had begun holding his head.

Before the King could restore order, a horse limped into the clearing.

The smaller animals moved hastily aside.

“He stole one of my shoes,” she neighed sadly.

She raised an unshod hoof for the gathering to inspect.

“I had four when I entered these grounds. When I left, I had three.”

Indignant voices rose from every side.

“Wine jugs!”

“A nest!”

“A shoe!”

Encouraged by the general ill feeling, the young mole lifted his bowl.

“And one of the children stood on my tail!”

His father slowly turned towards him.

The mole lowered the bowl.

“Although,” he added, “that particular matter may already have been resolved.”

The clearing descended into chittering, squawking and angry speculation. Some demanded that the gardener be banished. Others proposed confiscating his wheelbarrow. The rat suggested that his wine cellar should be searched as a matter of urgency.

Once again, the Elven King struck the ground three times with his ancient yew staff.

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

The noise subsided.

“We have heard the accusations,” he said. “Now—is there anyone here who wishes to speak for the gardener?”

A badger was the first to step forward.

“The gardener cares beautifully for these woods,” she said. “He leaves fallen branches where beetles may live, protects the entrances to our setts and never complains when we cross his paths with muddy feet. I am grateful to him, and I gladly call him my friend.”

Several badgers murmured their agreement.

The rat folded his arms.

“That does not explain my wine jugs.”

A fly rose from the gathering.

He flew to a tall mallow flower, placed his head inside its trumpet-shaped bloom and amplified his voice through the petals.

“Some of the finest poo in England ends up in that pond!”

The clearing became very quiet.

“Is that a defence?” the King asked.

“It is if you are a fly.”

The gardener had persuaded the Old Rectory to install a sewage system that used the pond as part of its filtration process. This arrangement was celebrated by the flies, regarded with mixed feelings by almost everyone else and never discussed while anybody was eating.

A fairy coughed delicately.

“The lilies are magnificent,” she said. “When the summer sun is at its fullest, their leaves provide shade for all the fairies beside the water.”

“And the poo?” asked the King.

“We try not to think about the poo.”

A blackbird landed upon a low branch.

“Tet tet tet tet!”

The animals fell silent. Blackbirds have never required mallow flowers to make themselves heard.

“Some of the finest trees and shrubs for many miles grow in these gardens,” she declared. “They provide food in winter, shelter in rain and excellent perches from which I sing my morning songs.”

Many birds called their approval from the branches.

A mole stepped forward next.

“The soil is rich, the lawns are extensive and the gardens are truly a delight to tunnel beneath.”

“Except where there is concrete,” said another mole.

A shudder passed through the entire mole delegation.

More creatures began to speak.

The hedgehogs praised the piles of leaves left beneath the hedges. The bees spoke of lavender, fennel and wildflowers. The frogs were grateful for the pond, although they declined to comment upon the filtration system.

The gathering’s anger began to soften.

The rat remained unconvinced.

“All very touching,” he said, “but he still stole my wine jugs.”

“And my nest,” Mother Wren added.

The horse raised her unshod hoof.

A hush descended upon the woods.

The Fairy Queen had entered the clearing.

She wore a gown of white petals sewn with drops of dew that shone like crystal sequins. Fairies moved aside as she passed, and even the rat lowered his paws from his aching head.

It was rare for the Queen to appear beyond the fairy circles.

The Elven King offered her his hand and led her to a place beside him. He returned to his seat, but she remained standing.

“I often visit the little room at the top of the gardens,” she told the gathering. “It is a peaceful place created by the humans, and a little of the Old Rectory’s magic gathers there.”

The animals listened.

“Three mornings ago, I sat beside its window while the gardener spoke to a group of children.”

The rat narrowed his eyes.

“On the table before him were three wine jugs, an old nest and a horseshoe.”

The clearing erupted.

“You see!” cried the rat.

The King raised his staff to strike the ground, but the Fairy Queen lifted one hand and silence returned immediately.

“The gardener showed the children each object,” she continued. “He asked them where it might have come from, who might once have used it and what its story could tell them about these grounds.”

She looked towards Mother Wren.

“The nest taught them to look carefully inside sheds, beneath roofs and among branches before disturbing anything.”

Mother Wren tilted her head.

The Queen turned to the horse.

“The shoe taught them that animals had travelled through these grounds long before the children arrived.”

The horse slowly lowered her hoof.

Finally, the Queen regarded the rat.

“And the wine jugs taught them that some woodland creatures possess habits which should not be examined too closely.”

The rat’s ruddy nose became considerably ruddier.

A squirrel began to laugh.

“The gardener did not use those objects to enrich himself,” the Queen said. “He used them to help the children notice the world around them. He taught them that every feather, footprint, seedling and discarded shell belonged to a story—and that they, too, had entered that story.”

The young mole looked down at the bowl in his gloved paws.

“Without children who recognise the magic of these grounds, there may one day be no adults willing to protect it. The gardener is helping to raise a generation that will care for the natural world we share.”

She looked across the gathering.

“Those objects were not taken from the woods merely to be possessed. They were given new purpose by someone who understood their value.”

For several moments, nobody spoke.

Then Mother Wren fluttered onto a lower branch.

“Did the children treat my nest carefully?”

“Very carefully,” said the Queen.

Mother Wren settled her feathers.

“Then perhaps the gardener may keep it a little longer.”

The horse nodded.

“And my shoe.”

Everyone looked at the rat.

“The jugs must eventually be returned,” he said.

“Empty?” asked the King.

The rat sighed.

“If absolutely necessary.”

The Elven King rose.

He struck the ground three times with his ancient yew staff.

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

Every creature in the clearing turned towards him.

“The Fairy Queen has reminded us of something easily forgotten,” he said. “These grounds belong neither to the human folk alone nor to the animals and small folk alone. We share them.”

He looked towards the young mole.

“The present gardener protects the woods, tends the pond and teaches children to value the living world around them. While he continues to care for our home, no creature shall deliberately undermine his work.”

A paw rose among the mole delegation.

“Does ordinary tunnelling count as undermining?”

The King considered this.

“Ordinary tunnelling shall remain protected under ancient custom.”

The moles sighed with relief.

“Vindictive tunnelling, malicious excavation and molehills shaped like rude gestures will not.”

The young mole concentrated upon his washing-up bowl.

“Furthermore,” the King continued, “we shall assist the gardener wherever we can. The birds shall spread his seeds. The worms and moles shall tend the earth beneath him. The fairies shall guard the wild places that human eyes overlook.”

He paused.

“And should the gardener ever forget that he shares these grounds with us, we shall remind him.”

The badger nodded.

The horse whinnied her approval.

Mother Wren fluttered her wings, and even the rat appeared satisfied—although this may have been because he had remembered where another wine jug was hidden.

The King unrolled a ceremonial scroll of Elven legal parchment. The proclamation covered matters of gratitude, mutual protection, tunnelling rights, acceptable quantities of mud and the correct procedure for reclaiming empty wine vessels.

He tied the scroll to a silver arrow, drew an ancient bow and fired.

The arrow crossed the clearing and embedded itself in the proclamation oak with a satisfying:

*Thwump!*

“Was the arrow strictly necessary?” asked the badger.

“No,” said the King. “But some traditions are worth preserving.”

With that, the meeting ended.

The following morning, the gardener sat beneath the proclamation oak during his break, drinking tea and eating a biscuit.

Sunlight streamed between the branches. A blackbird sang from the shrubs, bees moved among the lavender and the pond lilies spread their enormous leaves across the water.

The gardener felt an unusual calm in the woods.

He could not see the magical proclamation above his head. He did not know that the badgers called him their friend, that the birds had spoken in his defence or that the Fairy Queen had explained his work to the gathering.

Nevertheless, he felt that he was not alone.

He closed his eyes and silently thanked every creature in the grounds for allowing him to share their home.

High in the oak, the Fairy Queen sat upon a branch, her white-petalled gown shining in the morning light.

She smiled.

The animals and small folk were safe in the care of this man, and the gardener’s beloved grounds were safe in theirs.

Somewhere beneath his feet, a mole began digging.

The gardener opened one eye.

A small mound of freshly worked earth rose beside his boot.

He moved his tea out of the way.
