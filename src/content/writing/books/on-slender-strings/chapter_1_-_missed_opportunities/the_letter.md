# The Letter

Would Abi have avoided some complications if she had undergone surgery sooner? I will never know, and it would be wrong to claim a medical outcome that cannot now be proved. However, I was determined that another family should be spared having to endure a similar drama. So, not long after Abi arrived home from hospital, I wrote a hard-hitting letter to the manager of the local surgery.

The letter set out the sequence as I understood it then: repeated consultations; Abi’s worsening squint, headaches, vomiting and loss of mobility; the eventual recognition of raised intracranial pressure; and the emergency scan, transfer and surgery that followed. I contrasted the care she received after diagnosis with my anger that the seriousness of those earlier symptoms had not been recognised. I wrote:

> You were given many opportunities to spare her some pain, but it took someone outside your practice to recognise the seriousness of Abi’s condition and get her correctly diagnosed.

The original letter went further. Written while the fear was still raw, it accused the practice of failing Abi and said that, had she died, words such as “incompetence” and “negligence” would have felt justified to me. Those were my accusations and feelings at the time—not the findings of a court or inquiry. I include the substance of the letter not to pass judgement now, but to show how frightened and furious I was—and why I wanted those missed opportunities examined.

I also sent the complaint to my Member of Parliament and the Primary Care Trust. The case was reviewed and I was told that clinicians involved received further training in recognising unusual neurological signs and symptoms. That outcome mattered. I cannot know why I was told that symptoms like Abi’s were common. I only know that I did not feel reassured, and that she was subsequently found to be critically ill.

Children with brain tumours can be unwell for months before diagnosis. Greater awareness of the possible signs among front-line clinicians can shorten that delay and may reduce avoidable harm. That was the point of the letter then, and it remains the point of including it now.
