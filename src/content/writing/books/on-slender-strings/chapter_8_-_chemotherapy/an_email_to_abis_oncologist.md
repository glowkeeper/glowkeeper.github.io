# An Email to Abi’s Oncologist

A doctor came to discuss Abi’s nutrition. Her weight was again dangerously low and, even if she could keep food down, the doctor doubted that ordinary eating would restore what she needed quickly enough. She recommended either a nasogastric feed, which Abi had experienced before, or a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy—a tube passed directly into the stomach.

I was equally concerned about Abi’s weight, but reluctant to accept either intervention. That night, I emailed her oncologist. I explained that she had been vomiting every couple of hours, had barely eaten and had fallen to about 17.5 kg. I acknowledged the hospital’s concern but described the psychological effect the nasal tube had previously had on her, as well as my reluctance to put her through another surgical procedure. I asked whether we could first try a carefully monitored programme of high-calorie, high-protein food and supplements.

Her oncologist replied that nutrition was critical if Abi was to tolerate regular chemotherapy. He recommended setting a minimum acceptable weight and intervening if oral supplements could not achieve it. In his experience, children who needed tube feeding subsequently gained weight, spent less time in hospital with infections and generally adjusted to the decision. If Abi’s weight did not improve after the immediate illness had passed, he favoured a gastrostomy over a nasal tube because children tended to tolerate it better.

Soon after reading that response, I met with the doctors at our local hospital. Despite the oncologist’s suggestion, I was still keen to give Abi a chance at regaining weight naturally. After a lengthy meeting, we agreed on a four-day schedule that would steadily increase her calorie intake:

- Day 1: 352 calories
- Day 2: 652 calories
- Day 3: 952 calories
- Day 4: 1,252 calories

Day 1, Tuesday 28 April, started well. Abi managed to drink a whole Fortijuice, which just about met the required daily calories by itself. She then raced beyond the target by wolfing down a bowl of vegan jelly. Unfortunately, that was probably a bit ambitious because, at 5 p.m., she vomited it all back up. She became frightened, but a nurse put up fluids and Abi soon settled down and fell asleep. She slept all night. We both did.

Refreshed and ready for the challenge, we spent Day 2 keeping track of her food. She did well, managing to eat 932 calories. In hindsight, perhaps that was a bit much, but at the time she seemed to cope admirably.

I went home that night much more confident about my daughter’s health. The next morning, I arrived expecting to find Abi fit and well again, munching on breakfast. Unfortunately, I found her vomiting. She seemed fine afterwards, though, so I took her to the hospital schoolroom, where she happily played for a while and took occasional sips of a nutritional shake.

By the end of the session, Abi was beginning to tire. She looked grey and barely had the energy to reach the lift. Back in her room, she complained of a headache and later vomited again. Sarah agreed that she was becoming poorly, so we decided to stop trying to make her eat. I told a doctor that I now felt Abi needed a gastrostomy after all, but arranging the procedure could take a week or more.

The immediate plan was therefore to put a nasal tube into Abi’s intestine. She hated those tubes and took the news badly, saying in her distress that she did not want to be there any more. It was a fleeting moment, and she settled after a nurse gave her more anti-sickness medicine.

Poor child. Abi’s illness was turning into a very bumpy marathon. But not an unbearable one; we would get the nasal tube sorted and overcome whatever her disease had in store for her next.
