# The BFG

In 1960, Roald Dahl’s baby son Theo was seriously injured when his pram was struck by a taxi. Theo developed hydrocephalus and needed a shunt to drain cerebrospinal fluid from his brain. The valve repeatedly blocked, requiring further treatment and placing him at renewed risk.

Dahl took the problem to Stanley Wade, a precision hydraulic engineer, and Kenneth Till, the paediatric neurosurgeon caring for Theo at Great Ormond Street. Together they developed the Wade–Dahl–Till valve. Its moving steel disc was designed to be less vulnerable to blockage than the slit valves then in use, and its stainless-steel construction allowed it to be sterilised and produced relatively cheaply.

The valve entered production in 1962 and was used in an estimated two to three thousand children before newer designs superseded it. Theo’s hydrocephalus had stabilised by then, so he never needed the device his illness had inspired.

It is a small piece of medical history, but it belongs here. A frightened father encountered a problem in his child’s care and helped bring together the people who could make something better. I can see why that story stayed with me.
