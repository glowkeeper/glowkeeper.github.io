# Glossary

These are plain-English explanations of terms used in the book. They describe how the words are used here and are not a substitute for advice from a clinical team.

**Astrocyte**  
A type of support cell in the brain and spinal cord. An astrocytoma is a tumour whose cells resemble astrocytes.

**BRAF**  
A gene that helps control cell growth through the MAPK signalling pathway. Different alterations involving BRAF can behave differently and respond to different drugs.

**BRAF V600E**  
A specific change in the BRAF gene. Some gliomas with this mutation can be treated with medicines that inhibit BRAF and MEK.

**Blood–brain barrier**  
A protective boundary between the bloodstream and brain tissue. It can make it difficult for some medicines to reach a brain tumour.

**Carboplatin**  
A platinum-based chemotherapy drug. It has often been used with vincristine for paediatric low-grade glioma.

**Cerebellum**  
The part of the brain at the back of the skull that is especially important for balance and coordination.

**Central nervous system (CNS)**  
The brain and spinal cord.

**Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)**  
The clear fluid surrounding the brain and spinal cord. A blockage to its flow can cause hydrocephalus.

**Chemotherapy**  
Treatment using medicines that kill cancer cells or slow their growth. Different drugs have different aims and side effects.

**Complete resection / gross-total resection**  
Removal of all tumour visible to the surgeon or on postoperative imaging. It does not guarantee that no microscopic tumour cells remain.

**Dissemination**  
Tumour cells spreading from the original site to another part of the brain, spinal cord or cerebrospinal-fluid pathways. It does not mean that a new, unrelated tumour has formed.

**Endocrine system**  
The glands and hormones that regulate growth, puberty, metabolism and other body functions. Tumours or treatment near the hypothalamus and pituitary gland can affect it.

**External ventricular drain (EVD)**  
A temporary tube used to drain cerebrospinal fluid from the brain into a container outside the body.

**Glioma**  
A broad family of tumours arising from cells of the brain or spinal cord. Gliomas range from relatively slow-growing to highly aggressive.

**Histology**  
The study of tissue under a microscope. Modern tumour diagnosis often combines histology with molecular testing.

**Hydrocephalus**  
A harmful build-up of cerebrospinal fluid in or around the brain, usually caused by impaired flow or absorption.

**Hypothalamus**  
A small, vital area near the centre of the brain involved in hormones, appetite, temperature, sleep and other basic functions.

**KIAA1549::BRAF fusion**  
A rearrangement joining parts of the KIAA1549 and BRAF genes. It is the most common molecular alteration in pilocytic astrocytoma and is distinct from BRAF V600E.

**Low-grade glioma**  
A group of generally slower-growing gliomas, most often classified as CNS WHO grade 1 or 2. “Low-grade” does not mean harmless.

**MAPK pathway**  
A chain of signals inside cells that helps regulate growth and division. Alterations activating this pathway drive many paediatric low-grade gliomas.

**MEK inhibitor**  
A targeted medicine that blocks MEK, one component of the MAPK pathway.

**Molecular testing**  
Testing tumour tissue for genetic or other biological alterations that can refine diagnosis, prognosis and treatment choices.

**MRI (magnetic resonance imaging)**  
A scan using magnets and radio waves to produce detailed images of the brain and other tissues without ionising radiation.

**Nasogastric (NG) / nasojejunal (NJ) tube**  
A feeding tube passed through the nose. An NG tube ends in the stomach; an NJ tube continues into the small intestine.

**Oncologist**  
A doctor specialising in cancer. A paediatric oncologist treats children and young people with cancer.

**Overall survival**  
The proportion of people in a study who remain alive for a stated period after diagnosis or treatment, regardless of whether the tumour has progressed.

**Pilocytic astrocytoma**  
A usually slow-growing, CNS WHO grade 1 glioma occurring most often in children and young people. Its effects depend greatly on its location and whether it can be removed safely.

**Progression-free survival**  
The proportion of people alive without their tumour growing or returning during a stated period.

**Radiotherapy**  
Treatment using high-energy radiation to damage tumour cells. Modern techniques can reduce, but not eliminate, exposure of healthy tissue.

**Recurrence**  
The return of a tumour after a period in which it could not be detected or appeared controlled.

**Resection**  
Surgical removal of part or all of a tumour. The aim is usually the greatest removal that can be achieved safely.

**Shunt**  
An implanted tube-and-valve system that diverts cerebrospinal fluid to another part of the body, often the abdomen, to treat hydrocephalus.

**Targeted therapy**  
Treatment aimed at a particular molecular feature or signalling pathway in tumour cells.

**Tumour progression**  
Growth or spread of a tumour, usually demonstrated by scans, symptoms or both.

**Vinblastine / vincristine**  
Related chemotherapy drugs that interfere with cell division. Both have been used in paediatric low-grade glioma, in different treatment regimens.

**Watch and wait / active surveillance**  
Close monitoring with scans and clinical review instead of immediate treatment. Treatment can begin if the tumour grows or causes concerning symptoms.

**WHO grade**  
A classification describing a central nervous system tumour’s expected biological behaviour. Since 2021, diagnosis and grading increasingly combine microscopic and molecular features.
