# The Law of the Minusculus

It had been a hard day, a difficult night, and Mrs Nay had been working like a dog, so when Mr Nay entered her study with a cup of tea and a plate of biscuits, which he put on the desk, she was grateful for the respite. "Darling, you'll make your ear sore if you keep scratching it with your foot like that," said Godfrey, standing behind his tired wife, lovingly massaging her tense shoulders. Treesa put her foot back down, took her glasses off and rubbed her aching eyes. "It's this bloody sell-off of the Cabinet Office. The other lot just aren't keen; to make it appeal to the wider public, I have to find sneaky ways to gloss over the sordid details. It's taking all my energy!"

The sale had been gradual. The Cabinet was the last UK Government Office on Whitehall to be privatised. The least controversial sale was that of the Horse Guards to American defence contractors Heed LockMartin. After all, the general public liked their incredibly realistic AI Horsebots, not least because they were less prone to crapping on the pavement, which meant far fewer dry cleaning bills for unsuspecting passers-by. That's not to say that there were no dissenting voices. However, when one of the former Guard's horses romped home as 8/1 favourite in the nation's favourite death chase, most of the public forgot the scandal of her stablemates being sold off to France, where they appeared in the best Parisian restaurants, à la (horse) carte.

The assumption was that Heed LockMartin would be the successful bidder for the Ministry of Defence, too. Especially since their proposal to replace the nation's manned air force with drones had won widespread public support, given the success of the latest Horsebots parade. Imagine the controversy when, instead, the Iranian Government's defence arm, Hees Bollar, won the contract. Reading news of the purchase in the UK Government's publicity arm, the Daily Sale, Jack was incredulous: "Haven't we been at war with most of the Middle East for the last few hundred years?" he asked his wife, slamming the paper onto the kitchen table. "That's what I thought, too," Sally replied, "but Treesa explained her rationale during a speech to the Maidenhead Chamber of Commerce." She passed the paper back to Jack, pointing at the headline: "I'll Make a Lotta Your Money". According to the story accompanying the headline, by making Iran responsible for the weapons systems, it was unlikely that Iran would launch the UK's vast array of nuclear missiles at Iran. The same went for conventional weapons of mass destruction. Thus, Mrs Nay was confident that the deal made fantastic economic sense because it meant the UK was bound to use far fewer munitions. "See, it's all perfectly logical!" announced a satisfied Sally, who believed anything printed in the Daily Sale, especially when it made deferential reports of Mrs Nay's latest attempt to hoodwink the British public.

Fortunately for Mrs Nay, she had managed to bribe the Despotic Unionist Party. They would do anything for money because they belonged to the Cult of 1694 and worshipped at the altar of Threadneedle Street, so even though the Iranian deal was controversial, with the DUPed's support it wasn't too difficult to get through Parliament. The sale of the Cabinet Office and the Houses of Parliament was a different matter entirely. Because they were viewed as the last bastions of British democracy, the public were less keen to see them sold off to some global conglomerate. Worse for Mrs Nay, the Opposition were apoplectic.

"Order! Order!" demanded the Speaker of the House, Johan Burkus, resplendent in a ceremonial robe emblazoned with the logo of a large Chinese Hedge Fund, which sponsored the debating chamber in return for favourable deals (which amounted to licences to print money at the British public's expense). After the uproar had quietened, Burkus tipped the scales back in Mrs Nay's favour. "Prime Minister!" he crowed, inviting her to speak.

"Thank you, Mr Speaker," she replied. "Mr Speaker, the Opposition's opposition to the sale of the Cabinet betrays the Marxist ideology that underlies all of their actions!"

Mention of the dreaded 'M...' word was designed to cause an uproar on the benches behind Mrs Nay. Members of her Front Bench took their cue by holding their noses (they did that whenever they caught a whiff of Socialism entering Parliament). Not one of them had ever read the Communist Manifesto, mostly because there wasn't room on bookshelves already groaning with copious volumes of Ayn Rand. This was a pity, since they would have discovered that Marx had foreseen, in uncomfortable detail, exactly what they were doing, and had not been in favour.

"Mr Speaker," Mrs Nay continued, "by opening this very chamber to the free market, we are celebrating years of progress made by converting once stuffy old public services, such as energy, social care, council housing, water, the railways, the NHS and the Prison Service, into productive private initiatives." Cheers rang out from the benches behind Mrs Nay. She continued, "Or does the Opposition deny the gains made? Does the Opposition deny that the unemployment rate is as low as it has ever been? Does the Opposition deny the steady growth rate over the course of this Parliament? To put it plainly, does the Opposition wish to condemn the same British public it pretends to champion, to years of stagnation?"

Mrs Nay sat down, looking smug.

Jeers and catcalls greeted Mr Bincor, the Leader of the Opposition, when he stood to reply. Burkus begrudgingly quietened the howling mob on the benches behind Mrs Nay, but the Leader of the Opposition was shouted down for "Socialist nannying!" the moment he said that "never have there been greater levels of inequality, endemic low pay, personal debt, homelessness, longer treatment waiting times or more prison suicides than there are now!"

And so it was that the Cabinet Office and the Houses of Parliament were sold off to Done4U. They didn't get everything their own way, however. The contract to run the Cabinet had to be put out to tender every eight years, and every four years, board members were to go up for sale, and the Prime Minister would be whichever member had made the highest net profit. Gross profit was considered, of course, but because it didn't allow for skilful manipulation of the figures through tax efficiencies via offshore hedge funds - an ability greatly admired by Mrs Nay's economic advisors - it was deemed too restrictive.

Having sold the British public down the river Thames, Mrs Nay entered the offices of Done4U fully expecting the reward of membership of the board, whereby she would be put up for sale and made the first-ever privatised Prime Minister. "Hello, Mrs Nay," said Gideon Hasbeen, HR Director at Done4U. He put down the document he had been reading, looked up from his desk, and continued, "It says here you have had a brilliant career in public service!" Mrs Nay smiled, confident that she was about to be given a prominent role within the company. Her shock was considerable when Hasbeen's tone changed dramatically. "Unfortunately for you, we despise public service here. Now get out!" Two burly security guards, hardened through years of beating up youth offenders at one of Done4U's notorious prison franchises, entered the office and stood either side of Mrs Nay. When she showed signs of protestation, Hasbeen gave an almost imperceptible nod to the guards, who promptly bundled Mrs Nay out of the offices and onto the street. "Now look here!" she demanded, affronted. Her days of entering the corridors of power, unimpeded, appeared over, but refusing to believe that, she attempted to walk back inside. When the impassive guards continued to block her way (and Done4U's police patrol arrived soon after), the message finally began to hit home: *had she, the Great Deceiver, been completely and utterly deceived?* Mrs Nay, who wasn't used to self-doubt, realised that the ugly answer was that she had.

Worse was to come a few months later when Mrs Nay's money ran out. Her husband left her, and her house was repossessed because she failed to make her mortgage payment on time, mainly due to the six-week delay in paying her unemployment benefit. And so it was that Mrs Nay suffered the ultimate humiliation: she became homeless and was forced to beg for handouts from generous passers-by.

Unfortunately for Mrs Nay, by that time, the Law of the Minusculus was in full effect (the irony was that, unbeknown to her, she had been its chief architect). The law held that just about everyone's outlook got reduced to nil, whereby the needs of the individual held sway over the needs of the many. The result was that people became greedy and uncaring and unwilling to pay any heed to anyone beyond their own nose, especially, as they saw it: "nasty street beggars." Hence, those 'generous passers-by' were few and far between. Among those who hurried past were Jack and Sally, who paused only to read the Daily Sale's front page ("Beggars: Is it Their Choice?"), and concluded that it was. Mrs Nay had to suffer all sorts of indignities: being spat at, kicked and verbally abused, and being forever hungry, cold, wet and lonely.

Fortunately for Mrs Nay, the fundamental laws are eternally balanced, so, set against the Law of the Minusculus, there was the Law of the Gargantuan, which soon began to take effect. The Gargantuan stated that the needs of the many held sway over the needs of the individual. It offered a broad perspective, whereby people gave generously to those in need. The dissenting voices to the sale of the Cabinet Office were governed by that law when they made Mr Bincor, the Leader of the now disbanded Opposition, Head of the UK's largest food bank. Done4U was angry about it, since they despised acts of charity as distortions of the free market, but not even they could stop the food bank operating: the burgeoning numbers who depended on it made the demand impossible to ignore. And the Law of the Gargantuan ensured that it had food to give.

It just so happened that Mr Bincor was touring the Maidenhead food bank when Mrs Nay first entered, dirty and desperate. "Mrs Nay?" asked Bincor, not sure if the defeated old woman he saw in front of him was the person whom he used to find so terrifying in the dispatch box. She was too tired to answer, so Bincor asked one of the volunteers if they could personally see to it that Mrs Nay was cleaned up, fed and offered somewhere safe to sleep. Afterwards, when he was sure that it was indeed Mrs Nay, he sat with her and held her hand while she cried. In fact, he cried too - the Law of the Gargantuan treasured empathy and held that you should love your enemy, especially when your enemy was utterly defeated.

She woke the next morning on a camp bed at the back of the food bank, under a donated blanket, with a bowl of porridge on a crate beside her. The porridge, she noted with professional detachment, was an inefficient use of oats. Then she ate every grain.

By the afternoon the queue was out of the door. Mrs Nay, handed a tabard and told to make herself useful, was stacking tins when she heard a familiar voice.

"I don't understand it," said Sally, shuffling forward with a carrier bag, while Jack followed with his hands in his pockets and his eyes on the floor. "It said in the paper this would make us all richer."

"It did say that," said Jack. "I never believed it."

"You said you did."

Mrs Nay's hands stopped on the tin she was holding. It was a tin of beans, and she held it very still, because she had remembered where she had seen the phrase before. The Law of the Minusculus had never been passed, exactly. It had been Clause 412 of the Public Obligations (Reduction) Bill, which she had drafted late one night, with a cup of tea going cold at her elbow: *the needs of the individual shall, in all cases, prevail.* It had seemed so reasonable at the time. It had tested so well with focus groups. And here it was, in a carrier bag, being handed a tin of beans by its author.

She put the tin in Sally's bag. Sally thanked her without looking up, which was how the public had thanked Mrs Nay for most of her career, and for the first time it hurt.

That evening she found Bincor stacking chairs. "I wrote it," she said. "The Minusculus. All of it. I thought it would set people free."

"I know," said Bincor, and he carried on stacking.

"I'm sorry," said Mrs Nay, which was a word that had never appeared in any of her speeches.

"I know that, too," said Bincor, and he handed her a biscuit. The Law of the Gargantuan asked no more than that, and no less.

Mrs Nay stayed. At first it was only to peel labels off tins in exchange for soup, then it was for the company, and then, somewhere around the second winter, it was because she had begun to see the faces behind the queue. Her talent for finding sneaky ways to gloss over sordid details turned out to be just as useful in reverse: nobody knew better than she where the Minusculus clauses had been buried, and one by one, with Bincor beside her and the Gargantuan behind them, she dug them up. When the public took Parliament back from Done4U, Mrs Nay was at Bincor's side, then at his table, and eventually, to the astonishment of the Daily Sale, at the Maidenhead register office, where she became Mrs Bincor. Godfrey sent a card, and an invoice for the biscuits. The days of Done4U and their ilk soon passed.

It had been a hard night, a stressful day, and Mrs Bincor had been working like a dog, so when Mr Bincor entered her study with a cup of tea and a plate of biscuits, which he put on the desk, she was grateful for the respite. "Darling, you've been working too hard. Please rest," said Jeremy, standing behind his tired wife, lovingly massaging her tense shoulders. Treesa took her glasses off and rubbed her aching eyes. "It's this bloody renationalisation of the railways. That Chinese Hedge Fund is still making things difficult..."
