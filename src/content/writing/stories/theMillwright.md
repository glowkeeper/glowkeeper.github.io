# The Millwright

The Guides came over a long ridge that had once formed part of an ancient hilltop fort on the South Downs. Ahead of them stood Jill, the old windmill; below, Pyecombe lay quiet in the deepening shadow of the valley.

"Right, girls," called Mikayla, their leader. "Let's clear a space and make camp before we lose the light."

While the older girls unpacked the tents, Mikayla moved among them, sorting crossed poles, tightening slack guy ropes and answering three questions at once. When one of the younger Guides struggled to drive a peg into the hard ground, Mikayla crouched beside her.

"Turn it slightly—there. Let the mallet do the work."

An hour later, all the tents were standing and some of the girls had begun preparing the fire.

"Evening," Mikayla called to an old man making his way along the ridge. "Beautiful spot, isn't it?"

The man stopped and looked beyond her to the newly pitched tents. "Mostly. But you'd be wise to move on before nightfall."

Mikayla glanced back at the camp. From where she stood, she could see the girls gathered around the first hesitant flames of the fire. "Why? It looks perfect to me."

"Normally, it is." The old man lowered his voice. "But tonight is the night the Millwright walks."

"The Millwright?"

He nodded towards Jill. "The millwright of that windmill—and his dog with him. The story goes that three wandering women once came to his door asking for bread. He turned them away, not knowing they were witches. They bound his soul to the mill and condemned him to wander, begging."

Mikayla smiled politely. She had heard enough local legends on Guide trips to recognise one when it arrived.

"On the last full moon before harvest," he continued, "the Millwright comes begging. Frightens folk into feeding him. Trouble is, he's too good at the frightening. Most run before they think to give him anything."

His eyes returned to the tents.

"And some," he said, "have been frightened clean out of their minds."

The last of the sunlight had slipped from the ridge. The old man seemed to notice it at the same moment.

"Like I said: if I were you, I'd camp somewhere else."

Before Mikayla could answer, he turned and hurried away along the path.

Mikayla watched until the path carried the old man out of sight.

*Well, he was a ball of fun.*

She looked towards Jill. Its sails stood black and motionless against the fading sky. Then someone called her name from the camp, and she turned her back on the mill.

"Who's hungry?"

Soon sausages and eggs were sizzling in pans while beans bubbled over the fire. Woodsmoke drifted across the ridge as the girls crowded around with their plates, talking over one another between mouthfuls. Afterwards came hot chocolate and marshmallows, some toasted golden and others reduced to blackened lumps that had to be beaten from their sticks.

When everything had been cleared away, Mikayla took out her guitar.

"Any requests?"

"Wonderwall!"

"Run!"

"Good Riddance!"

Mikayla laughed. They all knew the handful of songs she could play, just as she knew they would sing every word whether they could hold a tune or not.

She played an E minor seventh chord. "Today is gonna be the day…"

Later, as the fire settled into a red glow, Mikayla looked around the circle.

"Who knows a good ghost story?"

No one volunteered.

"All right, then. Here's one I heard earlier."

With a few embellishments, she told them about the Millwright, the three witches and the curse that bound him to Jill. When she finished, the easy chatter around the fire had disappeared. Beyond the circle of light, the windmill was no longer visible.

One of the younger girls broke the silence. "How does he frighten people?"

Mikayla hesitated. "The old man didn't say."

The girl drew her blanket closer around her shoulders. Several of the others were watching Mikayla now, waiting for reassurance.

"Listen," she said, smiling. "He was only trying to frighten us—and by the look of you, he succeeded."

A few of the girls laughed.

"Come on. Bedtime. There's no such thing as ghosts."

Hours later, a crash tore through the camp.

Mikayla sat bolt upright. For a moment she remained in her sleeping bag, listening as the last metallic clatter died away.

"What was that?" one of the girls called from another tent.

Mikayla found her torch, pulled on her boots and stepped outside. Its beam swept across the remains of the fire before settling on the camp table. The pans and plates they had left there to dry were scattered across the ground.

Tent zips opened around her.

"Stay where you are," she called. "It was probably a fox looking for breakfast."

"Did it find any?"

"Not unless it likes washing-up water."

A ripple of laughter passed between the tents. Mikayla waited until the zips closed again, then crossed to the table and began gathering the cookware.

A long wooden creak sounded behind her.

She stopped.

Another creak followed, slow and deliberate. Mikayla turned her torch towards Jill. The mill's sails were moving.

She lifted her face, expecting to feel the breeze, but the air was perfectly still. Around the camp, the tent walls hung slack and the grass showed no movement.

Something pale caught the edge of her torchlight.

Footprints.

Mikayla crouched and touched one. A fine white powder clung to her fingertip.

Flour.

The prints crossed the grass from the direction of the mill. Beside them ran a second, smaller trail—the unmistakable marks of a dog's paws.

Together they led to Mikayla's tent and disappeared beneath its closed flap.

For several seconds she could do nothing but stare. Then she tightened her grip on the torch, crossed the camp and reached for the zip.

"Hello?"

No answer.

She pulled the flap open. A breath of air, cold enough to sting her face, rushed out past her.

Mikayla directed the torch inside. Her bag had been turned out and her clothes lay strewn across the groundsheet, each one dusted with flour.

A movement behind her made her turn.

At the edge of the torchlight stood an old man in a flat cap. A terrier waited at his heel.

The beam trembled in Mikayla's hand. For an instant she thought she could see the dark shape of the mill through the man's body.

Then both he and the dog were gone.

Mikayla remained where she was, sweeping the torch across the empty grass. Nothing moved. Even the sails of the mill had stopped.

*A trick of the light,* she told herself. The old man from the ridge had returned—that was all. He had frightened her with his story, disturbed the camp and searched her tent while she was distracted.

The explanation did not account for the footprints passing beneath the closed flap. Mikayla chose not to consider that.

She checked the other tents. The girls were quiet again, their shapes dimly visible through the canvas. Waking them and leading them across the Downs in darkness would only put them at greater risk.

Back inside her own tent, Mikayla searched through the scattered belongings. Nothing appeared to be missing except a packet of biscuits.

She took out her phone and called the police.

"Someone has entered my tent and searched through my things," she told the operator. "I also saw a man close to the camp."

"Is he still there?"

Mikayla looked through the open flap. "No. I don't know where he went."

"Has anything been stolen?"

"Only some biscuits, as far as I can tell. But I'm responsible for a group of young girls. We're camped beside Jill windmill, above Pyecombe, and I need to know that he's gone."

"Stay together and remain in your tents. We'll send someone to check the area."

"Thank you."

Mikayla ended the call and sat holding the phone, listening to the silence outside.

Headlights appeared on the track below. Mikayla watched them climb the slope until a marked police car drew up beside the camp.

One officer came towards her. From the other side of the mill, his colleague appeared with a familiar figure walking ahead of him.

It was the old man from the ridge.

"We found him behind the mill," the first officer said. "Is this the man you saw?"

Mikayla shone her torch towards him. There was no mistaking his weathered face or the coat he had been wearing earlier.

"Yes. He came through here before sunset."

"I told you why," the old man said. "I came back to make sure the girls were safe."

Mikayla looked at the flour inside her tent, then at the camp table with its scattered cookware.

"He told me this place was haunted," she said. "He must have been trying to frighten us away. When that didn't work, he came back and searched my tent."

"No!" The old man struggled against the officer's grip. "The story is true. The Millwright walks tonight—I warned you."

"You can explain everything at the station," the officer said.

As they led him to the car, the old man twisted around to face Mikayla.

"You have to feed him!"

The car door closed on the rest of his warning.

"We'll take him down and establish what happened," the first officer told her. "Keep your phone close. If you see anyone else, call us straight away."

Mikayla thanked him. She watched the car descend towards Pyecombe until its red tail lights disappeared into the valley.

For the first time since leaving her tent, she felt she could breathe properly. There had been a man on the ridge, and now the police had taken him away. Whatever she thought she had seen in the torchlight, there was an ordinary explanation after all.

Before climbing back inside, Mikayla made one final circuit of the camp. She tightened a loose guy rope, checked that the cookware was secure and ran her torch across the outside of her tent, looking for tears or other damage.

The canvas was unmarked.

Mikayla put her tent in order as best she could, shaking flour from her sleeping bag and folding the scattered clothes back into her pack. She left her boots on and placed her phone and torch beside her pillow.

As she pulled the sleeping bag around her, a wooden creak travelled across the camp.

Then another.

The sails of the mill had begun to turn again.

Mikayla watched their shadows pass across the canvas of her tent. There must be a breeze higher on the ridge, she decided. The police had searched the area. The old man was gone. She was safe.

She repeated those facts to herself until exhaustion carried her into sleep.

"Miss?"

Mikayla opened her eyes. Morning light filled the tent, and someone was tugging gently at the flap.

"Miss, you need to see this."

Outside, the girls had gathered around the far side of Mikayla's tent. They parted as she approached.

Across the tent's canvas, two words had been smeared in a chalk-white paste:

**FEED ME**

Directly beneath the message sat one of the camp's blue enamel bowls, filled to the rim with flour. A finger had traced a single word across its surface:

**BREAD**

"Is it the Millwright?" one of the younger girls whispered.

Mikayla could feel them all watching her.

"No," she said. "It's somebody's idea of a joke."

"But the police took the man away."

Mikayla looked at the damp white letters. She had checked that canvas herself before going to sleep.

"I know," she said.

She made herself look away from the message.

"It's still somebody's idea of a joke. Pack your things—we're leaving."

Before allowing the girls to dismantle her tent, Mikayla photographed the message and the bowl with her phone.

The girls returned to their tents. Only when their attention had moved elsewhere did Mikayla touch one of the white letters.

The paste was still damp.

While the girls packed, Mikayla moved far enough away that they could not overhear her and called the police again.

"I'm calling about the man your officers took from Jill windmill last night. I need to know whether he's still in custody."

"Do you have an incident number?"

"No. The officers didn't give me one."

The operator took her name, mobile number and the location of the camp. Mikayla waited, watching the girls pull pegs from the ground and roll the tents into untidy bundles.

"I'm sorry," the operator said at last, "but there's no incident recorded at that location."

"There must be. I called shortly after midnight. Two officers arrived in a marked car and took an elderly man away."

"We didn't dispatch a car to Jill windmill last night. I can't find any incoming call associated with your number either."

Mikayla lowered the phone and opened her recent calls. The call she was making appeared at the top of the screen. Beneath it was the call she had made before leaving home the previous afternoon.

Nothing in between.

She raised the phone to her ear again. "But I spoke to someone."

"If two people represented themselves as police officers, we need to treat that seriously. Are they near the camp now?"

Mikayla looked towards the track where the red tail lights had disappeared. In daylight, it seemed narrow and badly rutted. She wondered how a police car had climbed it so easily.

"No. They left hours ago."

"Stay with the group and make your way down to the village. Officers can meet you there."

"We're already packing," Mikayla said. "We'll leave now."

She ended the call and slipped the phone into her pocket before returning to the girls. She did not tell them that the first call had vanished.

The two officers were waiting beside their patrol car when the Guides reached Pyecombe. Mikayla moved the girls far enough away that they could not hear, then described the events of the night once again.

She showed the officers the photographs on her phone: the white words across the green canvas, the bowl beneath them, the flour disturbed by a single finger. She also handed them the bowl, which she had carried down in a clean plastic bag.

"And you're certain the men who came to the camp were police officers?" one of them asked.

"They were in uniform. They arrived in a marked car."

"At the top of the ridge?"

"Beside the mill."

The officers looked at one another.

"What is it?" Mikayla asked.

"The track beyond the lower gate is badly washed out," the other officer said. "We checked it before meeting you. A car wouldn't make it up there—not without leaving a great deal of itself behind."

Mikayla remembered the headlights climbing steadily towards her. She remembered the car drawing up beside the tents, its engine running quietly in the darkness.

"Perhaps there's another way up."

"There isn't."

A burst of speech came through the officer's radio. Mikayla flinched before she could stop herself.

"We'll examine the photographs and search the area," the first officer said. "For now, take the girls home. We'll contact you if we find anything."

Mikayla thanked them and returned to the group. The younger Guide who had asked about the Millwright was watching her.

"Was it a prank?" the girl asked.

Mikayla looked back at the two officers. One was speaking into his radio; the other was staring towards the ridge.

"That's what they're going to find out."

Two days later, with no word from the police, Mikayla went to the local studies library.

She told the librarian that she was researching Jill windmill. She did not mention the flour, the vanished phone call or the old man who had disappeared from her torchlight.

The librarian brought her a history of the Clayton windmills. Mikayla began with Jill, expecting little more than dates and diagrams.

She discovered that the mill had not always stood above Pyecombe. It had been built in Brighton in 1821, where it was known as Lashmar's New Mill, and moved to Clayton in 1852.

Something about the relocation troubled her.

"Would the same millwrights have looked after it in both places?" she asked.

"Not necessarily," the librarian said. "But we have the day book of a Sussex millwright from that period. There may be a reference in there."

She returned carrying a shallow archive box. Inside lay a leather-bound volume, its corners worn smooth and its pages crowded with small, precise handwriting.

The name written inside the cover was Jesse Pumphrey.

Mikayla worked slowly through the entries until she found a reference to Lashmar's New Mill. Pumphrey had recorded repairs to its machinery while it still stood in Brighton. Among the papers stored with the volume, she found a pencil portrait.

A lean man in a flat cap stood beside a windmill. At his heel sat a small terrier.

Mikayla stared at the face.

It was the man she had seen beside Jill.

Mikayla sat very still, trying to reconcile the fragile old portrait with the figure she had seen in the torchlight.

Her phone vibrated against the table, breaking her concentration.

She carried it into the corridor before answering.

The caller introduced himself as the Sussex Police officer who had met her in Pyecombe.

"We've completed our initial enquiries into what happened at the windmill," he said. "I'm afraid we haven't identified any of the men you described."

"What about the car?"

"There was no sign that a vehicle had passed beyond the damaged section of track. We also checked the telephone records. The only call your phone made to the police that morning was the one we have on file."

Mikayla tightened her grip on the phone. "I spoke to an operator during the night."

"I understand that's what you remember. But the call didn't pass through the network."

"And the bowl?"

"The bowl contained ordinary flour. We found no prints that could identify whoever handled it."

"So what are you saying?"

The officer was silent for a moment.

"I'm saying we can't explain what happened."

When the call ended, Mikayla remained in the corridor. Through the library doors she could see the old day book lying open on the table. The pencil figure seemed to be watching her from the page.

She returned to her seat and looked again at the history of Jill.

The mill had been moved several miles after Jesse Pumphrey worked on it. Yet the apparition had been waiting beside it at Clayton.

The old man's words returned to her:

*They bound his soul to the mill and condemned him to wander, begging.*

Not the place, Mikayla realized.

The mill.

She asked the librarian whether the collection contained anything about local ghosts.

After a short search, they found a slim volume entitled *Ghosts of Sussex*. Its account of the Millwright repeated the same story Mikayla had heard on the ridge: three women asking for bread, a refusal and a curse that followed Jill from Brighton to Clayton.

At the end of the chapter was the testimony of a woman named Agnes, recorded many years after she had encountered the Millwright.

Mikayla read:

*After that night, he came wherever I went. Sometimes I saw him across the street, with the dog beside him. Sometimes I only heard him. Three knocks at the door, always after dark. He is hungry still.*

According to the author, Agnes had become so terrified of unexpected sounds and unfamiliar faces that she could no longer live alone.

Mikayla closed the book.

The library around her seemed unnaturally quiet.

Mikayla left the library carrying nothing but her phone and the memory of Jesse Pumphrey's face.

Outside, dusk was gathering. Mikayla fastened her coat against the evening chill. The ordinary noise of the street felt almost reassuring: traffic passing, shop doors opening, fragments of conversation carried along the pavement. She told herself that whatever had happened at Jill was over. The mill was miles away.

She was halfway home when a voice spoke from the edge of the pavement.

"Any food, miss?"

Mikayla stopped.

An old man sat beneath a shop awning, a flat cap pulled low over his brow. Beside him was a small terrier.

The dog looked up first.

Then the man raised his face.

Mikayla saw the narrow features from the portrait, the same hollow cheeks she had glimpsed in the torchlight beside Jill.

"Just a little bread," he said.

She backed away. The old man made no attempt to follow, but his eyes remained fixed upon her.

Mikayla turned and ran.

At home, she locked the door and drew the curtains. She told herself that Agnes's story had prepared her to see an elderly beggar, an ordinary dog and a familiar kind of cap. Nothing more.

She remained in the hallway until her breathing slowed. By then, the last of the daylight had gone. She reached for the light switch.

Then came a knock at the door.

Mikayla did not move.

A second knock followed.

She remembered the words in *Ghosts of Sussex*.

Three knocks at the door, always after dark.

The third knock came.

Mikayla approached the door and looked through the spyhole. No one stood outside.

A soft scraping sound moved across the step.

She waited, one hand pressed against her mouth. When no further sound came, she fastened the chain and opened the door a few inches.

On the doorstep stood a blue enamel bowl, its familiar chip visible along the rim.

It was the bowl she had handed to the Sussex Police officers in Pyecombe.
