# The Druid

The light was already fading when Sam and Rachael reached the edge of the woods.

"How much farther were you planning to walk?" Sam asked.

Rachael slipped the map from the pocket of her rucksack and unfolded it across her knee. "There should be a clearing about a mile along this path."

"A mile?"

"You were the one who said we should keep going."

"That was before my feet lodged a formal complaint."

Rachael smiled and handed him the water bottle. "Come on. We'll have the tent up before dark."

The path led them beneath ancient oaks, their branches meeting high overhead. After the open slopes of the South Downs, the air among the trees felt unexpectedly cool. Ferns crowded the path and roots twisted through the earth beneath their boots.

"Listen," Sam said.

Rachael stopped. From somewhere in the canopy came a thin, descending call.

"Willow warbler," he said.

"Of course it is."

"You don't believe me?"

"I believe you've identified every bird we've heard since Lewes."

"Not every bird."

"You identified somebody's squeaking bicycle."

"In my defence, it was a very convincing great tit."

They found the clearing as the last sunlight withdrew through the trees. It was scarcely more than a break in the woodland, sheltered by oaks on every side and carpeted with last year's leaves.

Sam lowered his rucksack. "All right. I admit it. This was worth the extra mile."

"You can put that in writing later." Rachael began unfastening the tent from her pack. "For now, you can find us a patch of ground without roots."

By the time the tent was standing, the clearing had fallen into shadow. Rachael crawled inside to unroll the sleeping bags while Sam remained outside, listening to the woods settle around them.

A blackbird called somewhere among the trees. From the undergrowth came the dry movement of leaves as something small hurried past. High in the branches, wings beat briefly against the fading sky.

Then every sound stopped.

Sam waited for the blackbird to call again.

Nothing answered.

Even the leaves had become still.

Rachael emerged from the tent. "What are you listening to?"

"That's just it."

She looked around the clearing. "I can't hear anything."

"Woods don't fall silent like this—not all at once."

"Perhaps everything's gone to bed."

"Everything except us."

Rachael glanced towards the darkening path by which they had entered. For the first time, it looked less like a way through the woods than a narrow gap between the trees.

"Well," she said, "unless you want to walk another mile in the dark, this is still home for the night."

Sam searched the branches above them.

"A sparrowhawk may have passed overhead," he said. "That would quiet the smaller birds."

"And whatever we heard in the leaves?"

"Probably a fox."

Rachael looked into the motionless undergrowth. "Mystery solved?"

Sam listened again.

The silence held.

"Near enough."

As his unease receded, the hunger he had forgotten returned.

"What's for dinner?"

Rachael looked pointedly at his rucksack. "You were in charge of food."

"Then we're having two slightly flattened pasties."

"Is that all?"

"There's an emergency chocolate bar."

"Which you've already eaten?"

"Which I thought seriously about eating."

Rachael shook her head. "Put the kettle on."

Sam filled their battered camping kettle, screwed the burner onto its gas canister and tried the igniter.

Click.

Nothing.

He tried again.

Click.

The sound travelled sharply through the silent trees.

On the third attempt, a ring of blue flame caught beneath the kettle.

"Modern living," he said.

"I knew there was a reason I brought you."

They ate beneath the trees while the little stove hissed between them. The pasties were indeed flattened, but the tea was hot and strong. Afterwards, Sam divided the chocolate and handed Rachael the larger piece without being asked.

Their voices sounded strangely clear in the motionless air. When they had finished, Sam packed away the stove while Rachael stood brushing crumbs from her trousers.

A pale light had begun to show above the treetops.

"Look," she said.

The full moon rose slowly beyond the clearing, catching the highest branches before spilling between them. Shadows stretched across the ground towards the tent.

Rachael took Sam's hand. "Still think we chose the wrong place?"

He looked into the surrounding trees. "Ask me in the morning."

They zipped themselves into the tent, talked for a while and eventually fell asleep, worn out by the day's walk.

Sam awoke sometime later.

At first, he did not know what had disturbed him. The tent was cold, and moonlight showed faintly through the canvas.

Then, from deep within the woods, came a cry.

Sam held his breath, waiting.

"Rae?"

She stirred beside him. "What?"

"I heard someone."

They listened. For several seconds there was only silence.

Then the cry came again—closer this time—and ended in a choking gasp.

Rachael pushed herself upright. "What was that?"

Before Sam could answer, something struck metal outside. The blow rang through the clearing. Another answered it, followed by a confusion of distant voices.

Sam reached for his torch.

"Where are you going?"

"Someone might be hurt."

The voices grew louder. Men were shouting among the trees now, their words lost beneath the clash of metal and the pounding of feet.

An orange light moved across the tent.

For a moment, Sam thought someone else had entered the clearing with a torch. Then the light strengthened and began to flicker.

"That's fire," Rachael said.

Smoke found its way beneath the canvas.

Sam pulled on his boots and reached for the zip.

Rachael caught his wrist. "Don't."

"If the woods are burning, we can't stay in here."

He opened the tent.

Smoke rolled through the clearing, catching in his throat and stinging his eyes. Beyond it, flames climbed between the trees.

Yet nothing was burning.

The fire twisted around the trunks without touching them. Leaves remained dark and whole within the flames, and the ancient oaks stood undamaged beneath their crowns.

A figure rushed past the tent.

Sam recoiled. He glimpsed a bronze-coloured helmet, a rectangular shield and a short sword wet with blood. The man disappeared into the smoke before Sam could make sense of what he had seen.

Another shape staggered after him: an old man with long silver hair, dressed in a pale robe stained dark across the back.

He took two faltering steps and fell to his knees.

The hilt of a sword protruded between his shoulders.

"Sam?" Rachael called from inside the tent. "What can you see?"

He could not answer.

The kneeling man raised his head. For an instant, his eyes met Sam's.

Then someone behind him pulled the sword free.

The soldier stood over the fallen man, sword in hand.

Then he turned towards Sam.

Beneath the rim of his helmet, his face was grey and streaked with blood. His eyes found Sam's and held them.

Sam stepped backwards.

The soldier came towards him.

"Sam, get inside." Rachael's voice sounded very far away.

He tried to move, but his heel caught against the tent. Before he could recover, the soldier raised the sword in both hands.

Sam lifted an arm to protect himself.

The blade came down where his neck met his shoulder.

There was no impact.

No torn flesh. No blood.

The sword passed through him as though his body were made of smoke.

Cold opened inside Sam.

It was deeper than winter air or frozen water. It spread from his shoulder into his chest, following every nerve until even his teeth seemed to ache with it. His breath stopped. His hands became numb.

And the clearing changed.

Shapes stood among the trees where, a moment before, Sam had seen only smoke. Men, women and children watched from between the trunks, their clothes belonging to centuries he could not name. Some bore wounds. Others were grey with age or sickness. All of them were dead.

Their voices crowded into his mind: whispers, cries and fragments of words spoken long before he was born.

Rachael reached through the open doorway, caught hold of his coat and tried to pull him backwards. "Sam!"

He could no longer feel her hand.

The soldier drew the blade out through Sam's chest. The steel left no wound, but the cold remained.

He raised the sword again.

Behind him, the old man moved.

One hand closed around a dark wooden staff lying among the leaves. He pushed himself upright, and as he rose, the blood disappeared from his robe.

"Enough."

The word was quiet, but it carried across the clearing.

The battle faltered.

The soldier swung towards him. The old man lifted his staff, and the sword struck the wood without a sound.

For a moment, neither moved.

Then the flames bent towards the staff. Smoke streamed after them, drawing itself away from the trees. The soldier's body thinned into darkness and was pulled backwards with it, his sword vanishing last.

Across the clearing, the fighting receded. Shouts became whispers; running figures faded between the oaks. One by one, the fires went out.

When the last of the fighting vanished, the old man remained.

Behind him, the other dead still watched from among the trees.

Rachael climbed out of the tent and turned around.

"Where's the fire?"

There was no smoke now. The leaves were untouched, and the air no longer burnt her throat.

Sam pressed a hand to the place where the sword had passed through him.

Rachael saw the movement. "Are you hurt?"

He did not answer.

She pulled aside the neck of his coat and examined his shoulder. There was no wound and no blood.

When she touched his skin, she drew her hand back.

"You're freezing."

Beyond her, the old man came towards Sam, leaning upon his staff. His pale robe was unmarked now.

"Who are you?" Sam asked.

Rachael turned to follow his gaze. "Who are you talking to?"

"She can neither see nor hear me," the old man said.

Sam looked at the figures between the trees. "But I can see all of you."

Rachael took hold of his hands. "Sam, look at me."

He tried, but the dead were gathering behind her.

Rachael released him and took out her phone. "I'm calling for help."

She walked towards the centre of the clearing, watching the screen as she searched for a signal.

"The blade opened your sight to the dead," the old man said.

"Can you stop it?"

"I stopped the second blow. I cannot recall the first."

The whispers pressed more urgently into Sam's mind. He covered his ears, but the voices came from within as much as from the woods.

"Please. Help me."

The old man studied him.

"At sunrise, follow the path to the village. I will meet you where the woods end."

"Why not tell me now?"

A cry echoed far away among the trees.

The old man turned towards it.

"Because this night is not yet over."

He began to fade. The trees showed through his robe, then through his skin, until only the staff remained standing in the clearing.

That disappeared too.

"There's no signal," Rachael said behind Sam.

He turned. She was watching him with the phone still in her hand.

"We need to leave."

Sam looked towards the path. Dead faces watched from either side of it.

"Not in the dark."

"You said the woods were burning."

"I don't know what I saw."

"You were talking to someone."

"The path isn't safe."

Rachael looked towards it. "It's empty."

"Please, Rae."

She looked at his unmarked shoulder, then at the dark path.

"Inside, then," she said. "We leave as soon as it's light."

She led him back into the tent and wrapped both sleeping bags around him, but nothing warmed the cold beneath his skin.

"What did you see?" she asked.

"I don't know."

It was the first lie he told her.

Rachael stayed awake beside him for as long as she could. Eventually exhaustion overcame her, and her breathing settled.

Sam remained awake.

Shadows crossed the moonlit canvas. Some moved slowly between the trees; others stood motionless outside the tent. Their whispers continued until the darkness began to thin.

At the first suggestion of dawn, Sam eased himself from beneath the sleeping bags.

Rachael stirred but did not wake.

He dressed quietly, opened the tent and stepped outside.

The dead were waiting for him.

They lined the path through the woods, silent now, all facing towards the village.

Sam followed them.

The path brought Sam out of the woods and onto a narrow lane.

The village was waking. Curtains opened. A car started somewhere out of sight. Outside the shop, a delivery driver was stacking crates onto the pavement.

An elderly man stood in the middle of the road.

Sam stopped.

A car rounded the bend and drove straight through him.

The man remained where he was.

Sam turned back towards the woods. Figures stood beneath the trees all along the path he had followed. He could see them clearly in the morning light now. None of them cast a shadow.

"Sam."

The old man from the clearing was waiting beside the last tree.

Sam went to him. "What has happened to me?"

"The sword did not cut your body," the old man said. "It cut through the boundary between you and the dead."

Sam looked back at the man in the road. "Can they see me?"

"Some can."

"Can they speak to me?"

"Some will try."

"And how long does this last?"

The old man did not answer.

"How long?"

"For as long as you live."

Sam pressed a hand against the place where the blade had entered him. There was no wound, but the cold remained beneath his skin.

"Can't you undo it?"

"If I could, I would have done so."

"Who was the soldier?"

The old man looked into the woods.

"He came with others to destroy the grove and kill those who gathered there. I was among them. When that moon returns, so does the slaughter."

"Every year?"

"For nearly two thousand years."

Sam looked more closely at him. "What are you?"

"A keeper of that place, once."

A figure had appeared farther down the lane.

For one terrible moment, Sam thought it was another of the dead. Then it called his name.

Rachael.

She ran towards him, her coat pulled over the clothes in which she had slept.

Sam moved to meet her. She threw her arms around him and held on.

"I woke up and you were gone."

"I'm sorry."

"What happened last night? You were freezing. You kept staring at things that weren't there."

Rachael drew back and searched his face.

"Tell me what happened."

Sam looked at her. He wanted to tell her about the fire that had not burnt, the sword passing through his body and the dead who had watched the tent until morning.

"There was a soldier—"

"Stop."

The old man had not raised his voice, but Sam heard him clearly.

Along the lane, the dead began to turn towards them.

The man through whom the car had passed looked directly at Rachael.

Beneath the trees, one pale face turned, then another.

"What is it?" Rachael asked.

Sam looked from her to the gathering dead. "They can hear me?"

"They hear the blade's story being told."

"A soldier what?" Rachael asked.

The dead man stepped out of the road and came towards her.

Rachael shivered and pulled her coat more tightly around herself.

"She can feel him already," the old man said.

Sam moved between them. "If I tell her what happened?"

"Then your story becomes hers."

"She'll see them?"

"As you do. And once the dead have been given a place in her sight, they will never leave it."

The figure stopped only a few feet from Rachael. His gaze remained fixed upon her, though she could not yet see him.

"Sam?" she said.

He could see the fear returning to her face.

"What were you going to tell me?"

The dead man took another step towards her.

"I thought I saw a soldier in the woods," Sam said.

Rachael stared at him. "A soldier?"

The figure was close enough now that its face almost touched hers.

"But there wasn't one," Sam said quickly. "I was wrong."

The dead man stopped.

For several seconds, nothing moved. Then he turned away from Rachael and walked back towards the road.

Beneath the trees, the other faces began to lose interest.

"Careful," the old man said. "A story need not be finished to invite them closer."

"What do you mean, you were wrong?" Rachael asked. "We both heard those people. We saw the fire."

"I know."

"There was smoke inside the tent."

"I know."

"But nothing had burnt. You were freezing, and you spent the rest of the night staring through the canvas. Now I find you out here talking to somebody I can't see."

Sam glanced at the old man.

"Who were you talking to?" Rachael asked.

"Myself."

The lie remained between them.

Rachael looked towards the last tree, following Sam's gaze. The old man stood only a few feet away, but she saw nothing.

"You don't expect me to believe that."

"No."

"Then tell me the truth."

"I can't."

"Can't or won't?"

Sam looked at the dead lining the road. Some had turned away, but others were still watching Rachael.

"I can't," he said again.

Rachael watched him touch his shoulder. "Is it still cold?"

"Yes."

"There was nothing there when I checked."

"I know."

"That doesn't mean nothing happened."

Sam lowered his hand. "We should get away from here."

"That's the first sensible thing you've said."

Rachael turned back towards the woods.

The path was still crowded with the dead.

Sam hurried after her. "Stay close to me."

She gave him a searching look but took his hand.

As they entered the trees, the dead moved aside to let them pass.

Neither spoke until they reached the clearing.

In daylight, there was no sign of the night's violence. The leaves were unburnt. The earth bore no footprints, and the air smelt only of damp soil.

They packed without discussing what had happened.

When they were ready, Rachael shouldered her pack.

"Will you ever tell me?"

Sam opened his mouth.

At the edge of the clearing, the old man stood watching him. Beyond Rachael, pale figures waited between the trees.

Sam shook his head.

He could not make himself look at her.

"All right," she said.

She turned and started down the path.

Sam followed.

After a few steps, Rachael slowed until he was walking beside her.

Around them, the woods remained silent.

Sam never told Rachael what he had seen.

In all the years that followed, he never told another living soul.

Yet here is his story.

The old man had warned him that the dead hear when the blade's story is told. They have been listening all this time: through the fire, the first stroke of the sword and every word Sam could not bring himself to say.

Now the story is yours.

Before you go, listen.

Can you hear anything beyond your own breathing?

If the world around you has fallen silent, it is already too late.
