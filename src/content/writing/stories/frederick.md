# Frederick

*Adapted from a story made up by my eldest daughter on a train in 2012. The original is preserved below, exactly as she told it.*

Once upon a time there was a little boy named Frederick, and for thirteen years nothing was expected of him, which suited everyone.

At thirteen he was sitting on a bench with his friends, doing what thirteen-year-olds do on benches, which is nothing, at length, with great commitment. A monkey arrived. Nobody remarked on it. It is surprising what people will accept once they have decided not to be involved.

The monkey looked at Frederick, then at a scroll, then at Frederick again, in the manner of a man checking a passport.

"Could you please excuse me for a while?" said Frederick to his friends, who waved him off without looking up. It was the politest thing he had ever said, and it would turn out to be the last thing anyone asked him for a long time.

"The legend is foretold," said the monkey, "that you will slay the dragon. So off you go! Shew! Shew!"

Frederick was confused. He had been braced for many things, but not for a legend, and certainly not for one that had been foretold without consulting him. "But where am I going?" he exclaimed.

"Ah, good question," said the monkey, as though it were the first intelligent thing anyone had said to him all year. "You are off to slay the dragon! Here's a map."

The map was beautiful. It had a compass rose and a sea monster in the corner and a small, confident X. Frederick studied it for a respectful length of time, folded it, put it in his pocket, and lost it before he reached the end of the road.

He stood there a while with his hand in the empty pocket, feeling what every hero must feel at some point, which is that destiny might have been better organised. Then he remembered that he had an iPhone. The legend had not mentioned the iPhone, but the legend, he decided, was not the one in charge of the batteries. He asked the monkey, who had followed at a distance, for the dragon's coordinates. The monkey recited them without hesitation, which suggested he had been waiting to be asked. Frederick typed them into Google Maps, and the little blue dot began, patiently, to walk.

---

The way led first into a deep, dark forest, as ways in these stories do.

In the middle of it lived an evil witch, in a raggedy old hut with raggedy old curtains. Frederick stopped at the gate. One of the curtains twitched. He looked inside, and what he saw was neither a cauldron nor a skull but something worse: a room in which someone had been waiting a very long time for company, and had a plan for it.

"I shouldn't go in there!" said Frederick, and he did not go in there. He scarpered, quickly and without shame, until the trees thinned and he came out of the woods and onto a beach.

Years later he would count this among his finest decisions. At the time he merely felt that he had been rude to someone and got away with it.

On the beach he was met by vicious pirates. There were a great many of them, and they were vicious in the thorough, professional way of men who have done it for a living.

"Hehyah!" cried Frederick, and in one fell swoop of his sword the pirates were defeated. He was as surprised as they were. Nobody had told him he could do that; the sword, evidently, had been told, and had been waiting for him to catch up.

He took their ship, since it seemed a waste not to, and sailed for the island of the wood elves, which lay conveniently en route. It was full of beautiful trees and the sound of birds singing, and, beneath them, wood elves, who were partying. It was Christmas, after all. They were drinking fine ales and mead and singing songs that grew older and less decent as the evening went on.

The wood elves spotted Frederick. He could see the exact moment they did, because the singing stopped, and then began again, rather more warmly, with a tune that clearly had his name in it. They wanted him to stay. They would, he understood, want him to stay for a good many Christmases. He ran away before they could catch him, and he did not look back, because he knew that if he looked back he would have to stay.

Out on the open sea, in the middle of nowhere, a terrifying sea monster rose up in front of the ship, streaming water and fury. It looked exactly like the one in the corner of the map, only less decorative. It said nothing; it didn't need to. Frederick struck out with his sword and vanquished it, and he sailed on, wet and rather quiet.

---

When Frederick came at last to the shore of the island that was home to the dragon, he saw the mountain where it lived. There was no mistaking it. The mountain had been built to a dragon's specification, and all the other mountains nearby seemed to be keeping their distance.

It took him two hours to climb. When he reached the dragon's doorway it was ajar, which he found more alarming than if it had been barred. Somebody had left it that way on purpose. Somebody had been expecting him.

The dragon was waiting. But so was Frederick.

He drew his sword. The dragon, with a deathly breath of fire, did what dragons do, and Frederick, with his valiant sword, did what he had somehow become. They fought for many hours, long past the point where either of them could have said what they were fighting for. It had stopped being about the legend, or the monkey, or the map. It was now simply the thing that was happening, and each of them was trying very hard to be the one who was still standing at the end of it.

And as daylight broke, the dragon had been slain.

Frederick sat down beside it for a while. He had imagined something at this moment. He had imagined a trumpet, perhaps, or at least a feeling. Instead there was the cold, and the quiet, and the dawn arriving, regardless, over the sea. He looked at the dragon, which looked a good deal smaller now, and he said, to no one in particular, "Phew."

Nobody replied. It was the sort of achievement that really needed a witness, or at least a form to sign.

Behind the dragon, in the dark of the cave, lay a heap of gold. The legend had said nothing about expenses, and Frederick was a practical boy. He stuffed a few coins into his pockets, apologised to the dragon, and then climbed back down, clinking faintly.

---

He re-boarded his ship and sailed home, back past the island of the wood elves, whom he once more escaped. The music was still playing. It occurred to him that it had never stopped, and would not, and that this was the whole nature of the place. He went on across the seas and back through the woods, and once more deceived the witch, who watched him from behind her raggedy old curtains with the expression of someone who had been looking forward to the return journey rather more than the first.

Until at last he was back home, on the same road, in front of the same bench, where the monkey was waiting with a gold medal. He had, it appeared, been there the whole time. It was, the monkey announced, "Fit for a hero! Fit for a King!"

Frederick put it on. It was heavier than it looked.

When he saw his friends, he told them of his adventures and voyages across the seas: of the pirates and the sea warrior, of the dragon and the mountain and the dawn. They listened properly, which was not what he had expected. When he finished, one of them said, "You were gone *ages*," and another said, "Did you really kill a dragon?" and Frederick said that he had.

What he did not say, because he did not yet have the words, was that the dragon had been the easy part. He had been told where it was, and what to do about it. And the dragon, he suspected, had been waiting not to win but to be beaten: it had been very old, and very tired, and had left the door ajar for the one visitor who could give it a warrior's death, and with it a place in Valhalla, which does not take dragons who die in their sleep. It had been, in its way, a kindness. It was the witch's hut and the elves' music that he kept thinking about. He had been given a map to one place and had found, on the way, the doors to several others. He had walked past all of them. He had, in each case, been quite right to.

He sat on the bench, with the medal on his chest and the monkey sitting politely beside him, and the evening came down over the road, and he wondered, as heroes sometimes do, what exactly he had been sent to find.

And then he remembered the gold coins in his pocket.

## The Original
