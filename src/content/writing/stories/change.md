# Change

Old Man Harry lived high in mountains that were almost as ancient as the earth itself. He liked to know what each day would bring.

Every morning he woke at the same hour, washed in the same cold water and ate the same bowl of porridge. Afterwards, he brushed his teeth, settled into the comfortable chair on his veranda and lit his pipe.

From there, he watched the mountain awaken.

The jackdaws began cawing at breakfast time. The falcon made its first stoop shortly before noon, and the deer emerged to graze at the edge of the woods as the afternoon shadows lengthened. Harry knew these rhythms so well that he had little need of a clock.

Sometimes a tree fell during a storm or an unfamiliar bird appeared among the branches. Harry did not object to such things, exactly, but he preferred them to happen at a respectful distance.

“I’m too old for surprises,” he often told himself.

He had been telling himself this for a very, very long time.

Then, one bright, crisp winter morning, just as Harry lifted the first spoonful of porridge towards his mouth, somebody knocked at the door.

Harry lowered his spoon.

No one ever knocked at his door.

He waited, wondering whether the sound might have been a woodpecker, a falling branch or some other perfectly ordinary thing that did not require him to abandon his breakfast.

The knock came again.

Harry sighed, placed his spoon carefully beside his bowl and went to see who had disturbed the morning.

Standing on the doorstep was a rather plump man with a neatly trimmed moustache.

His shoes gleamed. His silver-topped cane gleamed. Even his freshly pressed overcoat appeared to gleam in the winter sunlight. Perched upon his head was a magnificent top hat.

“Harry!” the man cried.

This was Raphael, Harry’s city cousin: a gentleman accustomed to good company, fine food and the very best tea to be found on the whole Indian subcontinent.

“I have a few days between one or two extremely important meetings,” Raphael announced, bundling his way inside, “and I thought a breath of fresh mountain air would do me good.”

He took three steps into Harry’s home and stopped.

“Is this where you live?”

Harry looked around. There was his old table, his warm stove and the armchair in which he read on winter evenings.

“It is,” he said.

Raphael examined the room as if Harry had confessed to sleeping in a ditch.

And that was only the beginning.

During the days that followed, Raphael complained about the mud on his shoes, the wind beneath the doors and the mattress upon which he was expected to sleep. The mountain air was too cold, the paths were too steep and the nights were far too quiet. Even Harry’s home-cooked meals—of which Harry was rather proud—were insufficiently refined.

“You should visit the city,” Raphael told him repeatedly. “Every day brings something new. Concerts! Restaurants! Important people arriving from every corner of the world! A man must embrace new experiences, Harry.”

Yet when Harry invited him to watch the deer emerge from the woods, Raphael said it was too cold. When Harry suggested walking into the valley, Raphael was worried about his shoes. And when an owl called outside his window, Raphael spent half the night complaining about the noise.

Harry began to suspect that his cousin liked new experiences only when they came with polished floors and table service.

When Raphael finally departed, he was as glad to return to the city as Harry was to hear the end of his complaining.

The following morning, Harry woke at the usual hour, washed in the usual cold water and prepared his usual bowl of porridge.

The house was quiet again. No one complained about the temperature of the water or asked whether there might be something more interesting for breakfast. Harry carried his bowl to the table with a contented sigh.

Then he noticed a leather folder lying beside the bed in which Raphael had slept.

Across its cover, in Raphael’s large and elaborate handwriting, were the words:

**VERY IMPORTANT**

Harry opened it.

Inside were several pages covered with official-looking figures, an itinerary for Raphael’s journey and a letter confirming an extremely important meeting one week later. Harry understood very little of the figures, but he understood the words *must be presented at the meeting* perfectly well.

He looked at his porridge.

He looked at the folder.

Then he looked at his porridge again.

The post came to the mountain once a week and had visited only the previous afternoon. By the time the papers reached Raphael, his important meeting would be over.

Harry considered leaving the folder where it was. Raphael had forgotten it, after all. Perhaps a few days of confusion would teach him to take better care of his extremely important belongings.

But Harry knew he could not do that.

His cousin might be a dreadful guest, but he was still his cousin.

Harry ate his porridge before it went cold. Then he packed some food, put on his old mountain coat and boots and tucked the leather folder securely beneath his arm.

At the door, he stopped.

Beyond the veranda, the path descended through the trees towards the valley. Harry had walked that path many times, but he had never followed the road beyond it as far as the railway station.

He thought of the cold water, the comfortable chair and the deer that would appear at the edge of the woods that afternoon.

“I’m too old for surprises,” he said.

The folder waited beneath his arm.

Harry closed the door behind him.

By midday, he was standing on a railway platform with a ticket in his hand and a great black engine hissing in front of him.

The engine was considerably larger, louder and less respectful than any surprise Harry had encountered before.

Porters hurried along the platform. Doors slammed. A whistle shrieked so suddenly that Harry nearly dropped the folder.

For a moment, he considered turning around.

Then the conductor called for everyone to board, and Harry remembered Raphael’s meeting.

He climbed into the nearest carriage.

The train gave a great shudder and began to move.

Harry gripped the edge of his seat as the platform slipped away. The station roof vanished, the town gave way to open fields and the railway carried him farther from his mountain with every turn of its wheels.

*Tickety, tackity, wickety-wack. Clickety, clackety, sickety-sack.*

Harry loosened his grip.

Valleys opened beyond the window. Rivers flashed beneath iron bridges, sheep scattered from the trackside and distant hills rose in shapes he had never seen before.

A railway attendant brought him a cup of tea. Two children across the aisle laughed whenever the train swayed around a bend, and before long Harry found himself laughing with them.

He had expected the unfamiliar world to feel strange.

It did.

To his considerable surprise, Harry discovered that strange could also be wonderful.

By late afternoon, the train carried Harry into the largest city he had ever seen.

Buildings rose on either side of the tracks. Chimneys crowded the rooftops, bridges crossed above bridges and more people hurried along a single platform than Harry had expected to meet in an entire lifetime.

Harry checked into a small hotel near the station. He intended to eat supper, go directly to bed and continue his journey in the morning.

Instead, he found himself wondering where all those people had been hurrying.

He placed Raphael’s folder safely beneath his pillow and went for a walk.

The city was noisy, certainly. Carriages rattled over the streets, shopkeepers called from their doorways and crowds swept Harry along whether or not he had decided where he wished to go. But the noise was not meaningless. It was made from a thousand lives being lived at once.

Harry passed bakers, booksellers, tailors and shops whose purposes he could not begin to guess. Then he stopped outside a barbershop.

Through the window, he watched a barber draw a shining razor across a customer’s cheek. With each careful stroke, a different face appeared beneath the white lather.

Harry touched his beard.

It had grown uninterrupted for so many years that he could no longer remember precisely what lay beneath it.

“Only one way to find out,” he murmured.

A bell jingled as he entered the shop.

The barber stared at him.

“That,” he said at last, “is the finest beard I have ever seen.”

Harry was rather pleased to hear it.

“Thank you.”

“A little tidying around the edges?”

Harry settled into the chair. “I should like you to shave it off.”

The barber looked horrified.

This pleased Harry even more. Until that moment, he had not realised that a surprise could be something one gave to somebody else.

Once the barber had wrapped a cloth around his neck and lifted the scissors, however, Harry’s courage faltered.

“Will it grow back?”

“Almost certainly.”

“Almost?”

The barber smiled. “Hold still.”

Locks of grey hair tumbled to the floor. The scissors gave way to warm lather, and the lather gave way to the soft scrape of the razor. Harry kept his eyes firmly closed until the barber announced that the work was finished.

Then he looked into the mirror.

An unfamiliar old gentleman looked back.

Harry turned his head. The gentleman did the same. Harry raised one eyebrow. So did he.

“Well,” Harry said, “there you are.”

He had expected to feel as though something had been taken from him. Instead, he felt the cool air upon his cheeks and discovered a face he had not seen for decades.

He left the barbershop smiling.

The only disadvantage, he decided over supper, was that he could no longer save a little food in his beard for later.

The following morning, Harry retrieved the folder from beneath his pillow and made his way to the coach station.

The coach was smaller than the train but almost as intimidating. This time, however, Harry did not consider turning back. He climbed aboard, found his seat and placed the folder securely upon his lap.

An elderly woman sat beside him.

“Good morning,” she said.

“Good morning,” said Harry.

“Travelling far?”

Harry glanced at her. On the mountain, everyone he knew already knew where he was going.

“Farther than I ever have before.”

“How exciting.”

“I haven’t decided whether it is exciting or alarming.”

“Most worthwhile journeys are both.”

She introduced herself as Martha. Before the coach had left the city, she and Harry were talking as comfortably as old friends.

Martha had visited places Harry had encountered only in books. She told him about forests where the trees were wider than houses, deserts in which the sand sang beneath the wind and northern seas that shone green beneath the midnight sun.

Harry told her about his mountain: the jackdaws at breakfast, the falcon before noon and the deer emerging as the afternoon shadows lengthened.

“It sounds beautiful,” Martha said.

Harry looked through the window at the unfamiliar country rushing past.

“It is.”

A film began playing at the front of the coach. Harry had never seen one before and watched in fascination for several minutes, but soon he turned back to Martha. The moving pictures were remarkable; her stories were better.

By the time the coach reached the next city, the long journey seemed to have taken no time at all.

Harry and Martha said goodbye on the platform.

“I hope you find what you’re looking for,” she said.

Harry thought of Raphael’s papers.

He had begun the journey knowing exactly what he was looking for.

Now he was no longer quite so certain.

The coach had brought Harry to a city built beside the sea.

After finding a hotel, he set out to explore. The streets here were broader than those of the first city, and the evening air carried the mingled smells of salt, smoke and unfamiliar spices.

Harry passed men in neatly pressed suits and women in dresses that flowed behind them as they walked. He admired them, but he did not feel ashamed of his mountain clothes. His coat had kept him warm through more winters than he could count, and his boots had carried him safely down the mountainside.

Then he stopped before a tailor’s window.

A dark green suit stood upon a wooden figure behind the glass. The waistcoat was patterned with golden leaves, and the cloth seemed to change colour whenever the light caught it.

Harry leaned closer.

He had never worn anything made simply because it was beautiful.

Inside, a thin man with a measuring tape around his neck looked Harry up and down.

“Can I help you, sir?”

“Perhaps,” Harry said. “Do you have anything that would make a man look entirely unlike himself?”

The tailor glanced at Harry’s newly shaven face.

“That depends. Who are you trying to deceive?”

“My cousin, if I’m fortunate.”

The tailor smiled. “Then you have come to the right place.”

He brought out bolts of cloth in blue, grey, green and burgundy. Harry ran his fingers across linen, wool, silk and velvet. Some felt too delicate. Others were so stiff that he wondered whether the suit could stand upright without anyone inside it.

Eventually, he returned to the dark green cloth from the window.

“That one,” he said.

The tailor measured Harry from shoulder to ankle. Harry stood with his arms outstretched while the tape passed around his chest, his waist and rather more places than he had expected.

“Do people in cities endure this every time they buy clothes?” he asked.

“Only if they wish them to fit.”

Harry considered this.

“Mountain coats are much less inquisitive.”

By the following afternoon, the work was finished.

Harry regarded himself in the mirror. The green jacket fitted neatly across his shoulders. Beneath it was the waistcoat patterned with golden leaves, a clean white shirt and a dark tie. The trousers fell in an unbroken line to his boots.

He looked splendid.

He also looked a little ridiculous.

To Harry’s surprise, he found that he enjoyed both sensations equally.

The tailor wrapped Harry’s mountain clothes carefully in brown paper.

“Shall I dispose of these for you?”

Harry took the parcel from him.

“Certainly not. A fine suit might impress my cousin, but I wouldn’t trust it halfway up a mountain.”

Outside, Harry discovered one further disadvantage to his new attire: the trousers had no convenient hole through which he could scratch his bottom.

Change, he decided, sometimes required sacrifice.

The following morning, Harry carried the parcel and Raphael’s leather folder to the harbour.

The ship towered above the quay. Ropes as thick as Harry’s arm held it against the dock, while sailors hurried between stacks of crates and passengers crowded onto the gangway.

Harry looked from the ship to the dark water below.

The train had travelled upon iron rails and the coach upon a solid road. The sea appeared to have no interest in staying still.

“I’m too old for surprises,” he murmured.

This time, the words made him smile.

He joined the passengers climbing aboard.

When the ship’s horn announced their departure, Harry stood at the rail and watched the city slip away. Gulls circled above the masts. Sunlight danced upon the water, and a fresh breeze tugged at his new jacket.

For the first hour, Harry was delighted.

During the second, the deck began to rise and fall beneath his feet.

By the third, he was leaning over the rail and deeply regretting his breakfast.

“First voyage?” asked a passing sailor.

Harry nodded unhappily.

“Keep your eyes on the horizon. It gives you something steady to trust.”

Harry fixed his gaze upon the distant line where the sea met the sky.

The ship continued to roll. His stomach continued to object. The horizon, however, remained exactly where it was.

Gradually, the sickness passed.

Harry stayed at the rail. Dolphins appeared beside the bow, racing through the white water thrown up by the ship. Farther out, an albatross glided above the waves without once seeming to move its wings.

He thought of the falcon above his mountain and wondered whether the two birds would understand one another.

The voyage was not comfortable. Harry could not pretend that he enjoyed every moment of it.

But as evening spread across the sea and the western clouds turned gold, he was glad that discomfort had not sent him back to his cabin.

Something did not have to be easy, he realised, to be worth experiencing.

The following morning, the ship entered another harbour.

Harry stood at the rail as domes, towers and crowded quays emerged from the mist. He felt tired, slightly unsteady and very pleased with himself.

At the hotel, he unwrapped his mountain clothes to make certain they had survived the voyage. His coat was safe. His boots, however, looked even more battered beside the tailor’s magnificent green suit.

Harry considered them.

There was nothing wrong with the boots. Their leather was cracked, their soles were worn and one of them had developed a hole precisely where his most troublesome bunion required fresh air—but they remained excellent mountain boots.

They were not, however, especially convincing footwear for a mysterious city gentleman.

Harry consulted Raphael’s itinerary. His flight departed the following morning, leaving him several hours to explore.

Before long, he found a shoemaker whose window displayed rows of polished shoes. Some were pointed, some were buckled and some appeared far too delicate to risk upon an actual street.

The shoemaker selected a pair of dark Italian brogues made from leather so soft that Harry scarcely felt them upon his feet.

“Very distinguished,” the shoemaker said.

Harry admired them in the mirror.

“Could I climb a mountain in them?”

“I sincerely hope you will not try.”

Harry bought the shoes.

He wrapped his old boots and placed them inside the parcel beside his mountain coat. He had no intention of exchanging one useful possession for another merely because their uses were different.

The next morning, Harry arrived at the airport wearing his green suit and carrying three things: the parcel containing his mountain clothes, the leather folder containing Raphael’s papers and a growing suspicion that almost anything might be interesting if one paid sufficient attention.

The aeroplane waited beyond the terminal windows.

It appeared much too heavy to fly.

Harry watched luggage being loaded into its belly. He watched passengers disappear through its narrow door. Then, without once considering turning back, he joined the queue.

He requested a seat beside the window.

As the aeroplane gathered speed along the runway, Harry felt himself pressed against the back of his seat. The ground rushed faster and faster beneath him.

Then it fell away.

Harry laughed aloud.

Fields became squares of green and brown. Roads narrowed into threads, rivers flashed like silver wire and whole towns became small enough to cover with his hand.

Soon the aeroplane climbed above the clouds.

Sunlight spread across them in every direction, transforming their white surface into a country of valleys, cliffs and shining plains. Harry pressed his face closer to the glass.

Somewhere below lay mountains, cities, forests and seas. From above, none appeared more important than another, but each belonged to the same vast landscape.

Harry thought of his own mountain. Seeing more of the world had not made it smaller in his memory. If anything, he understood its place within the world more clearly.

When the aeroplane began its descent, Harry was sorry the flight had passed so quickly.

Beyond passport control, the final city awaited him.

Harry checked Raphael’s address and set off on foot. On the way, he passed a hatter’s shop. A black bowler hat in the window caught his attention, and a display of walking sticks stood inside the door.

Harry entered wearing a beautiful green suit, polished Italian shoes and no beard. He emerged wearing the bowler hat and carrying an elegant silver-topped cane.

He inspected his reflection in the glass.

The old man from the mountain had disappeared completely.

Harry knew better.

By early evening, he reached Raphael’s house: a tall building with stone steps, shining windows and a brass door knocker polished almost as brightly as Raphael’s shoes.

Harry climbed the steps and knocked.

Raphael opened the door.

For one delicious moment, neither man spoke.

Raphael looked at the bowler hat, the cane, the Italian shoes and the fine green suit. He looked directly into his cousin’s newly shaven face.

There was not the slightest flicker of recognition.

“Good evening,” Harry said, lowering his voice. “I have travelled a very long way to bring Raphael something of considerable importance.”

Raphael straightened immediately.

“I am Raphael.”

“Then I believe I have found the correct house.”

Raphael’s eyes settled upon the leather folder beneath Harry’s arm.

“You had better come inside.”

He led Harry into an entrance hall larger than Harry’s entire home. A chandelier hung above them, and every surface appeared polished enough to satisfy even Raphael.

“You must have travelled on important business,” Raphael said.

“Very important.”

Raphael showed his distinguished visitor into a drawing room, gave him the most comfortable chair and ordered a tray of excellent cheeses and the finest Indian tea.

“You must stay the night,” he insisted. “I could not possibly send a gentleman of your standing in search of a hotel.”

Harry thought of the rickety bed Raphael had endured on the mountain and accepted the invitation.

That night, he slept in a vast guest room beneath a blanket softer than any material he had touched before.

The bed was magnificent.

Harry still preferred his own.

The following morning, still dressed as a mysterious city gentleman, Harry joined Raphael for breakfast.

Raphael’s house had a room for almost every activity. There was a drawing room for drawing, although Harry had seen no pencils; a music room containing an untouched piano; and a games room in which no one appeared to play games.

Breakfast, naturally, was served in the breakfast room.

Raphael was already seated at the long table when Harry entered. He rose at once for his important guest.

“I trust you slept well?”

“Extremely well.”

“And the room was comfortable?”

“The most comfortable guest room I have ever occupied.”

Raphael looked pleased.

Harry placed the leather folder upon the table.

“I believe you need this.”

Raphael’s expression changed the moment he saw his own handwriting.

He snatched up the folder and opened it. His eyes moved rapidly across the documents inside.

“Where did you find these?”

Harry removed his bowler hat.

“Beside the bed you complained about.”

This time, he used his ordinary voice.

Raphael stared at him.

Harry waited.

Recognition arrived slowly. Raphael looked at the shaven chin, the green suit and the golden leaves upon the waistcoat. Finally, he looked into Harry’s eyes.

“Harry?”

“Good morning.”

Raphael sank back into his chair.

“But your beard—”

“Shaved off.”

“Your clothes—”

“Made for me.”

“Those shoes?”

“Entirely unsuitable for mountains.”

Raphael looked again at the folder.

“You brought these all the way from your home?”

“They said they were very important.”

“They are. Without them, this morning’s meeting would have been a disaster.”

For once, Raphael appeared to have nothing more to say.

Harry helped himself to toast.

“Last night,” Raphael said eventually, “I gave you my finest guest room.”

“You did.”

“When I stayed with you, I complained about everything.”

“You did that too.”

“And you still travelled all this way to help me.”

Harry spread marmalade across his toast.

“You are my cousin.”

Raphael lowered his eyes.

“I thought you were an important gentleman.”

“So you kept telling me.”

“I treated you differently because of your clothes.”

Harry looked down at the golden leaves upon his waistcoat.

“They are very impressive clothes.”

Despite himself, Raphael laughed.

Then he looked more closely at his cousin.

“You really travelled here by yourself?”

Harry told him about the train and the children laughing across the aisle. He described Martha’s stories, the horrified barber and the tailor who found mountain coats insufficiently inquisitive. He told him about the dolphins beside the ship and the albatross gliding above the waves.

He also told him about the seasickness.

“That sounds dreadful,” Raphael said.

“It was.”

“But you continued?”

“The horizon helped.”

Raphael turned the leather folder over in his hands.

“I told you that a man must embrace new experiences.”

“Repeatedly.”

“Yet I could not endure a cold bedroom or an owl outside the window.”

“It was a particularly persistent owl.”

Raphael smiled, but the smile soon faded.

“I thought living in the city made me open to the world. When I visited you, I wanted your mountain to behave like the city.”

“The mountain has never been very obedient.”

A clock chimed in the hall.

Raphael gathered the papers and stood.

“My meeting begins in an hour. Will you still be here when I return?”

Harry considered the warm kitchen of his mountain home, his chair upon the veranda and the deer appearing at the edge of the woods.

“Until tomorrow morning.”

Raphael returned late that afternoon with the leather folder beneath his arm and an expression of enormous relief.

“The meeting went splendidly,” he announced. “Thanks to you.”

Harry was pleased, although he still understood very little about what the meeting had been for.

Over supper, Raphael asked more questions about the journey. This time, he listened to the answers.

When Harry rose the following morning to leave, Raphael was waiting in the entrance hall with a suitcase.

“Where are you going?” Harry asked.

“With you.”

Harry looked at the suitcase. It was almost as large as Raphael.

“I am returning to the mountain.”

“I know.”

“There will still be mud.”

“I have packed different shoes.”

“The mattress will still be rickety.”

“I shall try not to mention it.”

“And the owl?”

Raphael hesitated.

“I shall attempt to make peace with the owl.”

Harry looked at his cousin for a long moment.

“Why?”

Raphael glanced around the gleaming entrance hall.

“Because I should like to visit your home properly this time.”

Harry opened the door.

“Then you had better come along.”

For the second time in his very, very long life, Harry set out upon a journey.

This time, Raphael walked beside him.

The return journey followed the same winding route by which Harry had come: aeroplane, ship, coach and train.

Harry was curious to discover whether the world would look different when he travelled through it backwards.

In many ways, it did.

The aeroplane no longer seemed too heavy to fly. The coach seemed smaller than he remembered, and on the train he recognised the rhythm of the wheels before the train had properly left the station.

*Tickety, tackity, wickety-wack. Clickety, clackety, sickety-sack.*

Raphael, meanwhile, made discoveries of his own.

When their flight was delayed, he began to complain about the organisation of the airport. Then he noticed Harry happily watching the luggage carts weaving between the aeroplanes.

Raphael closed his mouth.

On the ship, he spent the first hour in a comfortable lounge. Eventually, he found Harry standing at the rail.

“There is an excellent dining room below,” Raphael said.

“There is an excellent horizon up here.”

Raphael looked at the empty sea.

“What does it do?”

“Nothing.”

Raphael waited for further explanation.

None came.

He remained at the rail.

By the time the sun began to set, neither man had spoken for almost an hour.

Raphael was surprised to discover that the silence had not required improving.

At last, the train carried them to the small station in the valley below Harry’s mountain.

The remaining journey had to be made on foot.

Raphael looked at the path.

Then he looked at his enormous suitcase.

“How far is it?”

“Far enough to make you wish you had packed a smaller suitcase.”

Raphael lifted it with both hands and followed Harry.

Halfway up the mountain, rain began to fall. Water ran between the stones, and the path softened beneath their feet. Raphael’s sensible travelling shoes sank into the mud.

He opened his mouth.

Harry waited.

Raphael pulled one foot free with a loud sucking noise. When he set it down, the other disappeared almost to the ankle.

“Different shoes?” Harry asked.

Raphael looked at the mud covering his trousers.

Then he began to laugh.

“Apparently,” he said, “I require different feet.”

Harry took one end of the suitcase and they carried it up the mountain together.

Evening was settling over the woods when they reached Harry’s home. The old table stood where it had always stood. The stove gave its familiar warmth and the floorboards creaked beneath their feet.

Raphael placed his muddy suitcase beside the door and looked around.

“Still where I live,” Harry said.

Raphael heard the gentle rebuke.

He looked at the old table, the warm stove and the armchair beside it.

“It is good to be back.”

That evening, Harry served a simple stew with thick slices of bread. Raphael ate two helpings and thanked him for both.

The mattress was still rickety.

Raphael mentioned it only once.

In the middle of the night, the owl began calling outside his window.

Raphael lay awake listening.

The bird called once.

Another owl answered from somewhere farther down the valley.

A third voice joined them from the woods.

Raphael had previously heard only noise. Now he began to notice the spaces between the calls and the way each answer came from a different part of the darkness.

The following morning, he asked Harry what the owls had been saying.

“I have no idea,” Harry replied.

“You’ve lived here all these years.”

“They weren’t talking to me.”

After breakfast, Harry settled into his chair on the veranda.

Raphael brought out another chair and placed it beside him.

For several minutes, he shifted, crossed one leg over the other, uncrossed it and checked the time.

“When do the deer arrive?” he asked.

“When they are ready.”

Raphael sighed.

But he remained in the chair.

The jackdaws quarrelled among the trees. The morning shadows shortened, and shortly before noon the falcon folded its wings and dropped towards the valley.

Raphael leaned forward.

“Did you see that?”

“I did.”

They stayed on the veranda until the deer emerged at the edge of the woods.

During the week that followed, Raphael walked into the valley, learnt to light Harry’s stove and discovered that mud came off his shoes more easily than he had imagined. He did not enjoy everything. Harry had never expected him to.

When the time came to leave, Raphael packed his enormous suitcase.

“Will you visit again?” Harry asked.

“If you will have me.”

“The owl may object.”

“The owl and I have reached an understanding.”

Raphael returned to the city.

In spring, he came back to the mountain.

That summer, Harry visited Raphael. He attended a concert, ate in a restaurant where the menu contained no words he recognised and slept once more in the magnificent guest room. He enjoyed the concert, remained suspicious of the restaurant and still preferred his own bed.

Neither cousin required the other to admire everything.

As the years passed, Raphael visited more often and brought less luggage. One spring morning, he arrived carrying a single suitcase and no return ticket.

He had sold the great house with its unused rooms, although he kept a small apartment in the city and returned whenever he wished to hear music, eat excellent cheese or drink the finest Indian tea.

On the mountain, he repaired the draught beneath Harry’s door but left the creaking floorboards alone. Together, the cousins replaced the rickety bed.

The owl continued to call outside the window.

Many, many years later, Harry sat on the veranda wearing his golden-leaf waistcoat over an old woollen shirt. His mountain boots were upon his feet, and the battered bowler hat shaded his eyes.

Beside him sat Raphael in a sturdy mountain coat and an immaculate silk scarf.

Shortly before noon, Raphael looked towards the sky.

“Here he comes,” he said.

The falcon folded its wings and stooped towards the valley.

Later, as the afternoon shadows lengthened, the deer emerged to graze at the edge of the woods.

Some things upon the mountain happened just as they always had.

But on the veranda, where once there had been one chair, there were now two.
